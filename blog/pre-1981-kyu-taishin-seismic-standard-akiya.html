Pre-1981 houses (旧耐震): what Japan's 1981 seismic-standard change means for a ¥2M akiya, and what a retrofit actually costs

Short answer: a house whose building permit (建築確認) was issued before 1 June 1981 was designed to the "old seismic standard" (旧耐震基準), which aimed only at not collapsing in a moderate quake. Houses permitted after that date follow the "new standard" (新耐震), and wooden houses permitted after June 2000 follow a further tightened standard. A pre-1981 akiya is not automatically unsafe, but you should assume it needs a seismic diagnosis (¥100,000–200,000, often subsidised) and possibly a retrofit (commonly ¥1,000,000–2,000,000 for a small wooden house) — and that lenders, tax breaks and insurers will treat it differently.

What changed on 1 June 1981?

The Building Standards Act enforcement order was amended after the 1978 Miyagi earthquake. The new standard (新耐震基準) requires a building to suffer little damage in a "medium" earthquake (roughly JMA intensity 5) and not collapse in a "large" one (roughly intensity 6-upper to 7). The old standard only addressed the medium case explicitly. The difference showed clearly in the 1995 Kobe earthquake, where a large majority of collapsed buildings were pre-1981.

A second change matters for wooden houses: from June 2000 the standard was tightened again (the "2000 standard"), adding requirements for foundation design to the ground, balanced placement of shear walls, and metal connectors at joints. Houses permitted between 1981 and 2000 are "new standard" but may still lack these details.

Which date counts — permit, completion or registration?

The standard is fixed by the date the building confirmation (建築確認) was issued, not the date the house was finished or registered. A house registered in early 1982 may have been permitted in 1980 and therefore be old-standard. Listings usually show the completion or registration year (築年月). To be sure:

  • Ask for the confirmation certificate (確認済証 / 建築確認通知書) or the completion inspection certificate (検査済証). Many old houses have lost them.
  • If lost, the municipality's building department keeps a register (建築計画概要書 / 台帳記載事項証明) showing the confirmation date, obtainable for a small fee.
  • Rule of thumb when nothing exists: a house completed before about spring 1982 is probably old-standard; one completed in 1983 or later is probably new-standard, but verify.

Note also that many rural houses were built without any confirmation at all (unconfirmed structures were common in areas outside city-planning zones), in which case there is no date to check and the house is treated as old-standard for all practical purposes.

How do I find out if the house is actually dangerous?

A seismic diagnosis (耐震診断) by an architect trained in the standard method produces a score, the 上部構造評点 (Iw), for a wooden house:

Score (Iw)Assessment
1.5 or moreWill not collapse
1.0 – 1.5Unlikely to collapse
0.7 – 1.0Some possibility of collapse
Below 0.7High possibility of collapse

Most municipalities subsidise or fully fund the diagnosis for pre-1981 wooden houses (typical owner cost after subsidy: ¥0–50,000; unsubsidised ¥100,000–200,000). Some towns require the applicant to be the owner-occupier, so buy first, then apply — or ask the seller to apply now. The diagnosis report also lists the specific weaknesses, which becomes the retrofit plan.

What does a retrofit cost?

Costs depend on the score, the size of the house and how much interior finish must be removed. Ranges reported by the Japan Building Disaster Prevention Association and municipal subsidy programmes for a typical single-storey to two-storey wooden house:

Cheaper partial options exist: a "safe room" (耐震シェルター) or a reinforced bedroom frame for ¥250,000–600,000 protects occupants without bringing the whole house to standard, and some towns subsidise these too.

What else changes because a house is old-standard?

  • Mortgage tax deduction (住宅ローン控除): for existing houses the rule was changed in 2022 so that houses registered from 1982 onward qualify automatically; older houses need a seismic-compliance certificate (耐震基準適合証明書) or equivalent proof (source: National Tax Agency, No.1214). Cash buyers of a ¥2M akiya rarely use the deduction, but it matters if you plan to borrow.
  • Registration and acquisition tax reductions: the reduced rates for existing houses use the same 1982 / compliance-certificate rule.
  • Earthquake insurance (地震保険): premiums are set by prefecture and structure, and a 10% "construction year" discount applies only to buildings built from June 1981; a compliance certificate can earn a larger "seismic grade" discount. Insurers may also refuse cover for buildings in very poor condition.
  • Loans: many banks and the Flat 35 scheme require the house to meet the new standard or have a compliance certificate. Old-standard akiya are overwhelmingly bought for cash.
  • Demolition vs retrofit: for a very cheap house, demolition (¥1,000,000–2,000,000 for a small wooden house, sometimes subsidised for dangerous akiya) plus rebuilding is an alternative — but check first that the lot can be rebuilt at all (saikenchiku fuka, control areas).

FAQ

Is every pre-1981 house dangerous?

No. Many are heavily built with plenty of walls and score above 1.0 on diagnosis. The point is that you do not know until it is measured, and the cost of finding out is small.

Can I live in the house before retrofitting?

Legally yes; there is no occupancy ban on old-standard houses. Owners of houses with very low scores are advised to sleep in a reinforced room or on the ground floor near an exit until work is done.

Does a retrofit increase the resale value?

It increases the buyer pool (loans and tax breaks become available) more than the price. A compliance certificate is a selling point; keep the diagnosis and construction records.

What about the 1995 and 2011 earthquakes — is a house that survived them proven?

Surviving a distant or moderate shaking proves little about a nearby strong quake. Past earthquakes can also have loosened joints and cracked foundations, which is one more reason to diagnose rather than assume.

Does Akiya Check tell me the seismic standard?
