Akiya in Japan's heavy snow areas (gosetsu chitai): what to check before buying (2026)

Short answer: Roughly half of Japan's land area is officially designated heavy snow country (豪雪地帯, gosetsu chitai), and a cheap akiya there can carry costs and risks that a price tag does not show. The designation is a government support label, not a building standard. What your roof must carry is set locally, by the building authority of each prefecture or city, and an older house may have been built to a lower figure. Before you buy, check the designation, ask what snow load applies to the address, have the roof and frame inspected, and get written quotes for winter snow removal and for keeping pipes safe. Do not rely on estimates from the listing.

What the official data says

The designation. The Special Measures Act for Heavy Snowfall Areas (豪雪地帯対策特別措置法) is the basis for national snow-country policy. According to the MLIT White Paper chapter on heavy snow area measures, 532 municipalities are designated as heavy snow areas, of which 201 are designated special heavy snow areas (特別豪雪地帯). Together they cover about 51 percent of the national land area, with special heavy snow areas covering about 20 percent. Those figures are from the 2020 edition of the white paper. MLIT's Regional Policy Bureau also publishes a municipality list, 豪雪地帯・特別豪雪地帯の指定 (as of 1 April 2026), a Japanese-language PDF arranged by region. That list is the document to check for a given town.

What the label means for you. The white paper describes the policy purpose as supporting regional development and living standards where snowfall holds them back, through measures such as securing transport, improving living conditions and securing people to handle snow. It is not a building code and does not tell you whether a given house is safe. "Special" status means heavier snow and more severe disruption, so expect harder winter access and higher snow-handling costs.

Snow load is set locally. Under the Building Standard Law Enforcement Order, the vertical snow depth (垂直積雪量) used to design roofs is determined by the local building authority. Two public examples show how much it varies. Hokkaido's published table lists values from 60 cm to 250 cm by municipality, with the highest in mountainous and inland areas, and notes that some municipalities have different values for specific districts. Niigata Prefecture designates almost all of its area (except Sado City and Awashimaura Village) as a heavy snow zone and sets a minimum unit load of 29.4 N per square metre for each centimetre of snow. Your agent cannot give you the figure from memory. Ask the municipality's building section which value applies to the lot.

The 3 things buyers miss

1. The roof was built to an older, possibly lower, snow figure

Local snow values are set by rule and can be revised, and an old farmhouse may predate the current table, or may have been modified (a heavier roof covering, a new solar panel, a roof extension). Niigata's rules also allow design reductions where snow is expected to slide off naturally or be removed, on the condition of good maintenance and the ability to remove snow quickly when danger appears. A vacant house may not meet that condition, because nobody is there to clear the roof. Ask the inspector to assess the roof frame, sagging ridges, sticking doors and sliding windows (possible signs of deformation) and past leaks. Our home inspection guide explains what a building inspection covers, and houses from before 1981 raise a separate structural question, covered in the pre-1981 guide.

2. Who clears the roof, the drive and the neighbours' side

Heavy snow areas depend on people to handle snow, and one of the policy aims listed in the white paper is securing them. An absentee owner has to pay someone. Also ask where roof snow lands. A roof that drops snow onto a neighbour's land, a shared lane or a public road can lead to disputes and, if someone is hurt, liability questions. Ask the agent which way the roof slopes, whether there are snow guards, and whether there has been any complaint. Roof snow removal is also physically dangerous work, which is a reason to hire a contractor rather than plan to do it yourself.

3. Winter access and utilities

A lane that is passable in summer may be buried for months, and a plough may not clear a private approach. Ask the municipal roads section whether the road is on a snow-clearing route (除雪路線) and who clears the private section. Check how the house is heated (kerosene, propane, wood) and, if you are away, how the water is drained. Hokkaido University guidance written for students in Sapporo states that pipes should be drained when the outside temperature drops below minus 4 degrees Celsius, using the 水抜き valve; see the university's note. A rural house also often has a septic tank; see our jokaso guide for the running-cost side.

Snow-country akiya banks: what to expect

Many snow-country towns run their own akiya banks, and conditions differ town by town, so read the municipality's own page and check each listing's plot, outbuildings and any attached farmland. Two regional guides on this site cover the process in detail: Hokkaido and Nagano. The Hokkaido guide includes a Cabinet Office estimate showing that tsunami and earthquake damage are worst on a winter night, because snow slows evacuation. Look up the address on the national hazard map too: a snow-country lot can still have landslide or flood zones.

Costs: what to budget, as items you must price

We could not find a reliable national price for snow removal, so no figure is quoted here. Costs depend on roof size, snow depth, access and whether the work is a one-off or a seasonal contract. Treat these as line items to price, not guesses:

  • Roof snow removal and snow clearing: get written quotes from two or more local contractors, for a one-off clear and for a seasonal contract.
  • Strengthening or reroofing: only after an inspection; ask for itemised quotes. The renovation cost guide shows how to read estimates.
  • Winter heating fuel and water protection: ask the seller for last winter's bills.
  • Municipal support: some municipalities run snow-clearing subsidies, but such schemes may be aimed at resident households rather than vacant houses or absentee owners. Check the town's snow-measures page and ask in writing whether a non-resident owner qualifies.

Checklist for a heavy-snow-area akiya

  • Find the municipality on MLIT's heavy snow area list and note whether it is "special".
  • Ask the building section which vertical snow depth applies to the lot, and when the house was built.
  • Commission a building inspection of the roof frame, with attention to deformation and past leaks.
  • Ask where roof snow falls, and whether it reaches a neighbour's land or a road.
  • Confirm the road is on a snow-clearing route and who clears the private approach.
  • Get written quotes for seasonal snow removal and ask for last winter's heating bills.
  • Ask how the pipes were drained and whether the water can be shut off.
  • Ask the town in writing which snow-related support, if any, applies to your situation.

FAQ

What is a gosetsu chitai (heavy snow area) in Japan?

It is an area designated under the Special Measures Act for Heavy Snowfall Areas, which exists to support regions where heavy snow holds back economic development and living standards. The designation is about government support policy; it does not tell you how strong a particular house is or what a building must withstand. Check the MLIT list of designated municipalities, then ask the municipality about the specific address.

How much snow must a Japanese house be built to carry?

There is no single national figure. The vertical snow depth used for roof design is set by the local building authority. Hokkaido's published table, for example, runs from 60 cm to 250 cm depending on the municipality, and Niigata sets its own table and a minimum unit load per centimetre of snow. An older house may have been built to an earlier or lower figure, so ask a qualified inspector to check the structure against the current local value.

Can I leave a snow-country akiya empty over winter?

It carries real risk. An unheated house gets no help from heat loss to shed roof snow, and unattended pipes can freeze. Before buying, find out who would clear the roof and entrance, what a seasonal contract costs, and whether the municipality has any rules or support for vacant-house snow removal. Get written quotes from local contractors.
