Buying a house on a private road in Japan: 私道負担, position-designated roads and the consent letters to ask for before you sign

Short answer: yes, you can buy a house that fronts a private road (私道), and many ordinary Japanese neighbourhoods are built that way. The risk is not that the road is private. It is that you may (a) pay tax or share costs on a strip of road you cannot build on, (b) depend on neighbours' written permission to cross or dig up the road for water, gas and sewer pipes, and (c) find that the road does not qualify as a legal road at all, which means the house may fall under the no-rebuild rules. Three documents settle most of this before you pay a deposit: the registry certificate for the road land, the municipality's road designation papers, and signed passage-and-excavation consent. Share-ready key points

  • A building lot generally has to touch a legal road at least 4 m wide along at least 2 m of its boundary (Building Standards Act, Art. 43) — a private road can qualify, but only in certain legal categories.
  • "私道負担" on a listing means part of the land you buy is road. That part cannot be built on, and may or may not be taxed.
  • Since 1 April 2023 the Civil Code (Art. 213-2) gives a right to install utility pipes across another person's land when there is no other way — but it still requires notice and compensation, so written consent is far cheaper.
  • Ask for: 道路位置指定 papers, the road's registry certificate, and 通行・掘削承諾書 (passage and excavation consent).

What "private road" means in a Japanese listing

In Japan "road" is a legal category, not just a strip of asphalt. Under the Building Standards Act (建築基準法), a house can only be built on land that touches a road as defined in Article 42, and Article 43 requires the lot to be in contact with that road for at least 2 m. Public roads are owned and maintained by the national government, prefecture or municipality. Private roads are owned by individuals or companies, often the households that live along them. What the official sources say

  • Road contact is a legal requirement for building: Building Standards Act, Arts. 42 and 43 (e-Gov). A lot that fails it is generally listed as 再建築不可.
  • Utility pipes across other people's land: the Civil Code since 1 April 2023 has Articles 213-2 and 213-3 on the right to install, and to use, utility equipment on another person's land when it is impossible to receive electricity, gas or water otherwise. The installer must choose the place and method that causes the least harm, must give prior notice to the owners, and must pay compensation for damage (Civil Code on e-Gov). Some municipal water departments now ask applicants to confirm they gave that notice, as in Wakayama City's water supply application guidance (Japanese PDF).
  • Passage over surrounding land: Civil Code Art. 210 gives the owner of a landlocked lot a right of way over surrounding land. It is a fallback with conditions, not something to plan a purchase around.
  • Property tax on roads: land used as a road open to the public can be exempt from fixed asset tax under local tax law, but the exemption depends on how the municipality classifies the road. Osaka City and Kobe City publish their criteria and require an application. In both, a gate, a toll, a "no passage" sign or private use can lead to the exemption being withdrawn.

Because each local authority applies these rules in its own way, treat the pages above as examples of how to read your municipality's version, not as the rule everywhere.

The 3 things buyers miss on private roads

1. The consent to dig is not the same as the consent to walk

A house on a private road needs water, drainage and often gas pipes to run under the road to reach the public mains. Rebuilding, renewing a pipe or connecting a new bathroom can require excavation. Agreements that only say "you may pass" (通行承諾) do not automatically allow digging (掘削承諾). Before you rely on a consent letter, check four points: it is signed by every owner of the road (or by a representative with authority), it covers pipe installation, repair and replacement, it states whether passage and digging are free of charge, and it is written to bind the successors of the signers. A letter signed by a previous neighbour who has since sold may be worth little. Our guide to questions to ask the agent has the wording to request.

2. Your share of the road may be in the price and in the tax bill

When the listing says 私道負担あり, a part of the stated lot area is the road. Ask for the area in square metres, and calculate the usable area separately. If the registry certificate for the road land shows several co-owners (共有), you may be buying a fractional share and becoming jointly responsible for repairs. Some municipalities exempt public-use road land from fixed asset tax, but only if the use meets their criteria and the owner applies. Ask the seller for the latest tax notice (固定資産税納税通知書) and whether the road part is listed as non-taxed. The broader cost picture is in our guide to fixed asset tax and acquisition costs.

3. The road may not be a legal road, or may not stay one

A lane that looks like a street can fail to meet Art. 42. Common problems are a lane narrower than 4 m with no setback agreement, a position-designated road that was later altered (a building built over the approved line, or a dead end made longer), and a road whose designation papers cannot be found. If the house itself was built before the current rules, you can usually keep living in it, but rebuilding may be restricted. If the road is only an agreement between neighbours with no legal designation, ask the building authority what status it has. The result decides whether a bank or insurer is comfortable, and whether the next buyer can rebuild.

Costs: what private-road issues can add

No official statistics cover these, so ask for written quotes rather than relying on generic numbers. The items that can create cost are:

  • Consent from co-owners: sometimes a payment ("sign-off fee", 承諾料) is requested when excavation is needed. This is a negotiation, not a legal charge; make it a condition of the contract that the seller obtains the consent.
  • Pipe connection or renewal: depends on the distance from the public main and whether the road must be dug up and restored. Get a quote from a designated water-works contractor (指定給水装置工事事業者) in the municipality.
  • Setback: land given up to widen a narrow lane lowers usable area. Ask the building authority to mark the setback line before you buy.
  • Road repair share: private roads are normally repaired by their owners. Ask if there is a residents' association or fee (管理費, 私道管理組合) and how repairs have been paid for.
  • Registry and legal fees: getting a registry certificate for the house and the road costs a few hundred yen each (see our guide to reading the registry); a judicial scrivener (司法書士) will charge more for drafting consent agreements.

Checklist before you sign

  1. Find the road in the listing: does it say 公道 (public road), 私道, 位置指定道路, or 2項道路? Ask the agent to confirm the type in writing.
  2. Ask for the width of the road and the length of the lot's frontage on it. Compare with 4 m and 2 m.
  3. Ask for the registry certificates (登記事項証明書) for the house, the lot and the road land. Look at who owns the road and in what shares.
  4. Ask for the 道路位置指定 papers, or confirmation from the building authority that the road is a legal road.
  5. Ask for signed 通行承諾書 and 掘削承諾書, and check they cover pipes, repair and successors.
  6. Ask where the water, sewer and gas pipes run. For water and sewer, the municipality's waterworks and sewer departments hold pipe records.
  7. Ask how the road is maintained and who has paid for repairs, and whether any fee is due.
  8. Check whether the 重要事項説明 (important matters explanation) lists the private road burden. Our guide to the explanation shows what the agent must cover.
  9. If a lot has no usable consent, write a contract condition that the seller obtains it before handover, or walk away.

How this should change your decision

  • Public road at the front, no road share: nothing special; skip this article's steps.
  • Position-designated road, you own a share, and consent letters exist: common and workable. Read the letters and put the share in your cost sheet.
  • Private road with no share and only a verbal arrangement: require written consent signed by all owners before the deposit, or treat the house as a rebuild-and-repair risk.
  • Road does not meet the width or frontage rule and no setback is possible: assume the lot is effectively no-rebuild and price it as a house with a limited life. See the no-rebuild guide.

FAQ

Can a foreigner buy a house on a private road in Japan?

Yes. Nothing in the road rules depends on your nationality. The same Building Standards Act and Civil Code rules apply to every buyer. Our guide on whether foreigners can buy akiya covers the wider question.

What if one neighbour refuses to sign the consent letter?

Do not buy on the assumption you can force it. The Civil Code gives limited statutory rights (Arts. 210, 213-2) that require notice and compensation, and disputes are resolved case by case. Make the signed consent a condition of the contract, or ask a lawyer who handles real estate disputes.

Will I pay fixed asset tax on the road part of my lot?

It depends on the municipality. Some exempt land used as a public-access road if the owner applies and the criteria are met (Osaka and Kobe publish theirs). Ask the seller for the current tax notice and the municipality's tax office for the status of the road part.

Does a private road affect getting fire insurance or a loan?

It can, if the road status raises doubts about rebuilding. Ask the insurer and, if relevant, the lender early. Our guide to fire insurance for akiya lists what insurers ask for.
