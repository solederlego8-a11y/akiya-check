Japan Property Sold Prices: How to Check Real Estate Transaction Prices (reinfolib Guide)

To check Japan property sold prices, use the government's free Real Estate Information Library (不動産情報ライブラリ, "reinfolib"), run by the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT). Search the district of your listing, pick the matching property type, and read the recent transaction prices, land areas, and building ages. Then convert the asking price to a price per square meter and compare it with those sales. The data comes from questionnaires, is shown only at town or district level, and is thin in rural areas, so it tells you a plausible range rather than a verdict. This guide shows the steps, the Japanese labels, and how to judge whether a cheap house in Japan or an akiya is fairly priced.

What the official data says

The reinfolib home page describes the site as an MLIT portal offering information on real estate transaction prices, land price indicators (公示地価), disaster-prevention data, urban planning, and nearby facilities. It has a Japanese/English language switch. The top page is at reinfolib.mlit.go.jp, and the English menu names the two relevant tools "Data Search/Download" under transaction prices and "Data Search" under land prices (English page).

MLIT's own explanation of the transaction data is on its real estate transaction price information page. It says the information is built from questionnaire surveys of real estate transaction parties (不動産取引当事者へのアンケート調査), that the location is given at town or district level (所在地（町・大字レベル）), and that the transaction price is shown to two significant digits (取引価格（有効数字２桁）). The same page reports about 5.47 million transaction records accumulated since April 2006, as of March 31, 2025. The reinfolib search page itself is titled "不動産価格（取引価格・成約価格）情報の検索・ダウンロード" (search and download).

Practical consequences: you will not see a street address or an exact sale price, and the figures are self-reported. MLIT's materials also describe the release as periodic, with new quarters added roughly every three months, so the newest sales may lag by several months. Check the site for the latest quarter it covers.

Step by step: finding sold prices for a listing

The screens are mostly in Japanese even on the English pages. The labels below are the ones to look for. Exact wording can change when MLIT updates the site.

  1. Open the transaction price search from the reinfolib top page (取引価格・成約価格 → データの検索・ダウンロード). A map view (地図) is also available if you prefer to pan to the area.
  2. Choose the area (地域): prefecture (都道府県), then city or ward (市区町村), and if offered, the district (地区). Take the district name from the listing address, for example the part after the city name.
  3. Choose the type (種類). The main options for our purposes are 宅地(土地と建物) (residential land with building, i.e. a used house and its lot), 宅地(土地) (land only), and 中古マンション等 (used condominiums). An akiya normally belongs under 宅地(土地と建物), or under 宅地(土地) if the listing is sold as land with an old house to be removed.
  4. Set the period (時期). Start with the last three to five years. In a rural town with few sales, widen it to the maximum available.
  5. Optionally filter by nearest station (最寄駅) where the area has one. Many akiya are far from any station, so treat this filter as optional.
  6. Read the result columns: 取引価格（総額） total price, 面積（㎡） land area, 建物の面積 floor area, 建築年 year built, 間取り floor plan, 土地の形状 lot shape, 前面道路 (road type, width), 都市計画 zoning, and 取引の事情等 remarks. The remarks column can flag things such as "訳あり" (special circumstances), which matter for interpretation.
  7. Download the results if you want to sort them in a spreadsheet, then calculate price per square meter.

Note that 前面道路 and 都市計画 are useful side checks. A narrow road or an urbanization control area often explains a low price, as covered in our guides to saikenchiku fuka (rebuilding prohibited) lots and urbanization control areas.

Reinfolib vs land price publication vs rosenka

Three different "land price" numbers get confused in English searches for Japan land price data. They answer different questions.

Transaction prices (取引価格情報)What buyers and sellers reported for real deals, from questionnaires. Includes houses, land, and condos. Best for "what do similar properties actually sell for here?"
Published land prices (地価公示 / 公示地価)Appraisal-based prices at fixed survey points, for land only. MLIT describes the published price as an assessment of vacant land (更地評価) and as an unbiased price (かたよらない価格) on its land price publication page. Good as a neutral benchmark for land, but the survey points are sparse and mostly urban. Prefectural land price surveys serve a similar purpose at other points, and reinfolib's land price section lets you search them.
Rosenka (路線価)A per-road value used to calculate inheritance and gift tax. The National Tax Agency describes it as the value per square meter of a standard residential lot facing a road (NTA No.4602). Maps are on rosenka.nta.go.jp. It is a tax valuation, not a sale price, so do not use it as a price target.

In short, published land prices and rosenka value land, not your particular house on it. Transaction prices are the closest thing to "sold prices" for a house with land, and they are the right first stop.

The 3 things buyers miss

  1. Old houses are often valued at almost nothing. A wooden house that is decades old is frequently treated as land with a building of little or no value. The transaction may appear under the house-and-land type but price out like bare land. Compare the asking price with land-only sales too, and remember that removing a building has its own cost and tax effects (see our fixed asset tax and acquisition costs guide).
  2. A few records can mislead. In a small rural town there may be only a handful of sales in several years. A single unusual deal, such as a sale between relatives, a house with defects, or a large lot, can pull the picture in either direction. Read the remarks column and never rely on a single data point.
  3. Sold price is not the same as risk. A fair price for a lot that floods, cannot be rebuilt, sits in a control area, or has a heavy septic or repair burden is still a risky purchase. Prices reflect the market's guess about these issues, and the guess may not match your plans. See home inspection costs for an akiya and listing red flags in kanji.

How to compare: making the numbers line up

  • Same district, same type. Compare 宅地(土地と建物) with 宅地(土地と建物), and stay within the listing's town or district before widening to neighbors.
  • Similar land area. A 100 m² lot and a 600 m² lot do not compare directly. Filter to roughly half to double your lot size.
  • Similar building age (建築年). Separate pre-1981 buildings (older seismic standard) from later ones. See our note on the pre-1981 seismic standard.
  • Convert to unit prices. Divide total price by land area to get yen per m². One tsubo (坪) is about 3.3058 m², so multiply yen per m² by 3.3058 to get yen per tsubo, the unit many Japanese listings use.
  • Look at the cluster, not the average. Sort the results and see where your listing falls between the lowest and highest sales, remembering the two-significant-digit rounding.
  • Add recency. Weight the most recent quarters, but do not throw away older data in low-volume areas.

A purely illustrative, made-up example: suppose an akiya lists at 8,000,000 yen on 200 m² of land, or 40,000 yen per m². If ten hypothetical nearby house-and-land sales cluster between 15,000 and 30,000 yen per m², the listing is above that range and it is worth asking why. If the cluster runs 35,000 to 50,000, the asking price looks in line. These numbers are invented to show the method, not real market data.

When the asking price is higher or lower than the market

Higher than comparable sales can mean the seller is anchoring on an old valuation, or that the property has real advantages: better road access, a flat and regular lot, recent renovation, or a desirable location. Ask the agent to justify it, and see our list of questions to ask the agent. Asking prices in Japan are often negotiable, particularly on properties that have sat unsold.

Much lower than comparable sales is a signal to look for the reason before celebrating. Typical causes are no legal rebuilding, a flood or landslide zone (see our hazard map guide), leasehold land, shared or private road access, structural damage, or an unmentioned disclosure item. Sometimes there is no reason beyond a motivated seller, but that should be a conclusion, not an assumption.

Costs and time

Reinfolib is free to use. Your time cost is a few hours the first time you learn the screens. Beyond the price, remember other one-off costs such as brokerage fees, registration, taxes, and inspection. Amounts vary widely by price and location, so treat any estimate as a range and confirm it with the agent and a licensed professional.

Sold-price check: a short checklist

  • Confirm the listing's district (地区) and choose the matching 種類 (宅地(土地と建物) or 宅地(土地)).
  • Pull at least three to five years of transactions; widen if fewer than about ten records appear.
  • Filter by land area and building year (建築年) to be close to your listing.
  • Convert everything to yen per m² (and per tsubo if the listing uses it).
  • Read the remarks and road columns (取引の事情等, 前面道路) for special circumstances.
  • Compare with land-only sales and with published land prices as a floor for the land.
  • If the asking price is far outside the cluster, write down the question for the agent, not a conclusion.
  • Check hazards, zoning, and road access separately: price does not tell you these.

FAQ

Is there an English version of reinfolib, Japan's real estate price database?

Yes. MLIT's Real Estate Information Library has a Japanese/English language switch. Menus and navigation are translated, but many result fields, place names, and notes remain in Japanese, so knowing the labels above helps.

Are the prices on reinfolib the actual prices people paid?

They come from questionnaire responses, not registry records. MLIT states that location is shown to town or district level and that prices carry two significant digits, so you get an approximation of the local market rather than an exact record of one sale.

How can I tell if an akiya listing is overpriced?

Convert the asking price to yen per m² or per tsubo and compare it with recent transactions in the same district, of the same type, with similar land area and building age. A gap is a reason to ask questions about road access, zoning, hazards, and condition, not proof of a bad deal.
