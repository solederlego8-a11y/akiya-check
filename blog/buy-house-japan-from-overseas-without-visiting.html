Buying a house in Japan from overseas without visiting: power of attorney, remote signing, payment and the 2026 Ministry of Finance report

Short answer: yes. Japanese law does not require a buyer to be present, resident or a citizen, and purchases by people who never set foot on the property happen every month. What changes when you buy remotely is that every step normally done across a table in the agent's office — the important matters explanation, signing, the identity check for registration, the hand-over of money — needs a substitute: a video call, a power of attorney, a notarised signature, a bank transfer from abroad. And because you cannot walk the lot, the checks you would normally do with your own eyes have to be done on paper and on official maps before any deposit is paid.

This guide walks through the remote purchase in the order it actually happens, lists the documents you will be asked for, and flags the one reporting rule that changed on 1 April 2026 and that most English guides have not caught up with yet.

Step 1: Do the due diligence that a visit would normally replace

A buyer on site picks up a lot without trying: the smell of damp, the width of the road, the slope behind the house, the river at the end of the street. A remote buyer gets none of that, so the paper checks carry the whole weight. Before you make an offer, make sure you have, for the exact address:

  • Hazard layers — flood depth, landslide zones and tsunami from the national Hazard Map Portal (重ねるハザードマップ). Our hazard map guide explains how to read each one, and the liquefaction guide covers the layer most buyers skip.
  • Zoning and rebuildability — whether the lot is in an urbanization control area and whether it meets the road-frontage rule, or is saikenchiku fuka (再建築不可).
  • The registry — the full certificate (全部事項証明書) for land and building, which shows the registered owner, mortgages, the land category (地目) and area. You can buy it online through the registration information service or ask the agent. If the land category says 田 or 畑, read our farmland guide first.
  • Construction year — anything built before June 1981 was designed to the old seismic standard (旧耐震).
  • Comparable prices — actual transaction prices nearby from the government's real estate information library (how to use reinfolib).
  • Boundaries and slopes — whether the boundaries are fixed (boundary guide) and whether there is a cliff or old retaining wall (retaining wall guide). These are the two things photos hide best.

Then ask someone to look at the building. A licensed inspector can do a visual inspection (建物状況調査) without you present and send photos and a written report; see home inspection in Japan. Ask the agent for a live video walk-through as well, and request specific shots: the underside of the floor from the inspection hatch, the ceiling under the roof, the base of the walls on the north side, the road in both directions, and the back of the lot.

Step 2: Offer, important matters explanation and contract — remotely

Once you decide, the agent will ask for a written purchase application (買付証明書 / 購入申込書). It is not binding, but it sets the price and conditions you are asking for. Put your conditions in writing at this point — for example that the seller will confirm the boundaries, or that the sale is conditional on an inspection result.

Before the contract is signed, the agent must explain the important matters (重要事項説明) through a licensed specialist and hand over the written statement. Since 2017 for rentals and 2021 for sales, this explanation can be given by video call (IT重説) as long as the documents are delivered in advance and the specialist shows their licence on camera; since the 2022 amendments to the Real Estate Brokerage Act the documents can also be delivered electronically with the buyer's consent. Ask for the PDF several days before the call so you can have it translated. Our juyo jiko setsumei guide goes through each section in English.

The sales contract (売買契約書) can then be signed in one of three ways:

  1. By post — the agent sends two originals; you sign and return them with the deposit transferred. Slow but common.
  2. Electronically — many agents now use e-contract platforms for sales contracts, which avoids revenue stamp duty on the contract as well.
  3. Through a proxy in Japan — you give someone a power of attorney to sign on your behalf.

The deposit (手付金), commonly 5–10% of the price, is paid at signing. Under the standard contract, after paying it you can withdraw only by forfeiting it, and the seller can withdraw only by paying back double, until the other side has started performing the contract. Read the contract's clauses on contract nonconformity (契約不適合責任): many akiya are sold on an "as-is" basis where the seller excludes this liability, which is legal between private parties and means problems found after hand-over are yours.

Step 3: The documents that replace your presence

The difficult part of a remote purchase is the registration of ownership. The judicial scrivener must confirm your identity and your intent to buy, and the registration needs proof of the buyer's address (住所証明情報). For a resident, that proof is a resident record (住民票); a non-resident foreigner has no resident record, so substitutes are used. (The seller, not the buyer, is the one who must provide a seal certificate.)

Practice differs between scrivener offices and between countries (some countries do not have a notary system that matches what Japan expects), so ask the scrivener for the exact list before you sign the sales contract, and allow two to four weeks to get notarisation and an apostille if needed. The settlement date in the contract should leave time for this — a scrivener who receives the documents a day late cannot register, and the seller may treat the delay as your breach.

Your name will be registered in katakana as well as the Roman alphabet. Since April 2024 the registry also records the Roman-alphabet name of a foreign owner, and the Ministry of Justice has introduced requirements for owners living abroad to register a contact in Japan (国内連絡先). The scrivener will ask for these details; check that the spelling matches your passport exactly.

Step 4: Paying from overseas

The balance is usually paid on settlement day (決済日) in Japanese yen into the seller's account, at the same time as the scrivener confirms the registration documents. Practical points for an international transfer:

  • International wires take one to several business days and the receiving bank deducts fees. Send the money to the account specified in the settlement instructions early enough, and agree with the agent and scrivener how a few hundred yen of shortfall will be handled.
  • Many buyers wire the funds to the scrivener's or agent's escrow-style deposit account (預り金口座) a few days before, so that settlement can happen on the day. Confirm the account name and number by phone or video with the office you know — not only by email. Payment-instruction fraud by email affects property sales everywhere.
  • Your own bank will need to know the purpose of the transfer. Keep the contract and the settlement statement (精算書) for your own tax records.
  • Japanese mortgage lending to non-residents is very limited, so most remote purchases are cash purchases. A loan from a bank in your home country secured on property elsewhere is the usual alternative.

Costs on top of the price: agent's commission (capped by law; for low-priced properties a special rule allows up to ¥330,000 including tax for houses of ¥8 million or less), the scrivener's fee and registration tax, revenue stamp on a paper contract, prorated fixed asset tax, and later the real estate acquisition tax. Step 5: The Foreign Exchange Act report — what changed in April 2026

Under the Foreign Exchange and Foreign Trade Act (外国為替及び外国貿易法), a non-resident who acquires real property in Japan must report the acquisition to the Minister of Finance, through the Bank of Japan, within 20 days of acquiring it (report form 22). The report is required regardless of the price or area — the Ministry's FAQ says even a ¥0 acquisition or an inheritance counts.

Until 31 March 2026, acquisitions for the non-resident's own residential use (or that of relatives or employees) were exempt. The Ministry amended the reporting ordinance with effect from 1 April 2026: for acquisitions on or after that date, the residential-use, non-profit and own-office exemptions apply only to rights relating to real property (such as a ground lease or other lease rights), not to the ownership of the property itself. In practice, a non-resident who buys an akiya now has to file the report even if they intend to live in it. The FAQ also states that a holiday home or second home is not "residential use" for this purpose.

Practical details from the Ministry of Finance overview and FAQ (Japanese):

  • The form and its instructions are in Japanese only, and the report must be written in Japanese. A resident agent (for example the real estate agent or scrivener) may file it for you, online.
  • A foreign national is generally presumed to be a non-resident unless they work at an office in Japan or have been in Japan for six months or more since entry. If you are unsure which you are, ask the Ministry or the agent filing for you.
  • Failing to report, or reporting falsely, is punishable under Article 71 of the Act (up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥500,000). If you missed the deadline, the FAQ says to file anyway with a short note explaining the delay.

Agree in writing who is filing the report and ask for a copy. It is easy for each side to assume the other is doing it.

Step 6: After hand-over — owning a house you are not living in

  • Tax agent: appoint a 納税管理人 so the fixed asset tax notice (sent around April–June) reaches someone who can pay it. Unpaid tax leads to arrears charges and eventually seizure.
  • Insurance: arrange fire insurance from the settlement date. Vacant houses are treated differently by insurers; see fire insurance for akiya.
  • Utilities: electricity can usually be switched online; water is applied for at the municipal waterworks, often by phone or form. If the house has a septic tank, maintenance contracts continue — see jokaso costs.
  • Someone local: an empty house deteriorates quickly. Arrange for a neighbour, the agent or a property-watch service to air the house and check after storms. Under the 2023 amendments to the Vacant Houses Act, a poorly kept house can be classed as a "managed-inadequate vacant house" (管理不全空家); if the municipality then issues a formal recommendation (勧告), the residential-land reduction on fixed asset tax is lost.

FAQ

Do I need a visa or residence card to buy a house in Japan remotely?

No. Ownership of land and buildings does not depend on nationality or residence status. A visa only matters if you want to live in the house for long periods, and buying property does not give you one. See can foreigners buy an akiya for the ownership rules.

Can the real estate agent sign everything for me?

An agent can act as your proxy under a power of attorney, but think about conflicts of interest: the agent is paid when the deal closes. Many buyers give the power of attorney to the judicial scrivener's office for registration only and sign the sales contract themselves by post or electronically.

Is the April 2026 reporting change a ban on foreign buyers?

No. It is a reporting requirement, not a permission. It widens the cases where a non-resident has to tell the Ministry of Finance about an acquisition after the fact; it does not stop the purchase or require approval in advance.

How long does a remote purchase take?

From accepted offer to settlement, a cash purchase commonly takes one to two months. Notarisation abroad, an apostille and the international transfer are the usual bottlenecks, so ask for the document list on day one.

What is the biggest risk of buying without seeing the house?

Paying a deposit before you know the things a visit would have shown you: that the road is too narrow to rebuild, that a cliff behind the house needs a wall, that the lot floods. All of these can be checked from official data for the exact address before you commit.
