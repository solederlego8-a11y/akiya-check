Fire Insurance (Kasai Hoken) for a Japanese House or Akiya: A Guide for Foreign Buyers (2026)

Direct answer: Fire insurance (火災保険, kasai hoken) is a private product, and the official sources cited below set no nationality requirement for it. What decides your outcome is the property: an old or long-vacant house can be refused or priced differently by some insurers, and earthquake damage is not covered by fire insurance at all. Earthquake insurance (地震保険) is a separate add-on with fixed limits, and flood cover (水災) is something you choose after checking the hazard map. Get quotes before you commit to a purchase, not after.

What the official data says

  • Earthquake cover is an add-on. The Ministry of Finance explains that earthquake insurance is contracted together with a fire policy, so having fire insurance is a prerequisite. The same page states that fire insurance does not pay for fire caused by an earthquake, or for damage from a fire that spread because of one.
  • Fixed ceiling. The earthquake amount is set at 30% to 50% of the fire insurance amount, capped at 50 million yen for the building and 10 million yen for household contents (Ministry of Finance; same figures on the General Insurance Association of Japan (GIAJ) earthquake insurance page). The system is meant to support recovery of daily life, so it will not necessarily pay for a full rebuild.
  • Seismic discounts exist. GIAJ notes discount schemes based on a building's seismic isolation or earthquake-resistance performance. Whether an older house qualifies depends on documentation, which links to the pre-1981 seismic standard issue.
  • Water-disaster pricing is location-based. The General Insurance Rating Organization of Japan (GIROJ) publishes a lookup by municipality that sorts locations into five water-disaster classes (1 lowest risk, 5 highest). GIROJ says the class-5 water-disaster premium is about 1.5 times that of class 1, and the data on that page is dated 21 June 2023.
  • Duties to disclose and notify. GIAJ's consumer Q&A on fire insurance covers the policyholder's duty to report accurate facts at signing (告知義務) and to tell the insurer when conditions change (通知義務). Building use and occupancy are the kind of facts that matter here.

The 3 things buyers miss

1. Vacancy and building age change the offer

An akiya is, by definition, often unoccupied. Insurers treat an empty house differently from an occupied home, and an old house with no recent maintenance may be handled differently again. Some insurers may decline, restrict the cover, or classify the building under a different category. GIAJ's Q&A suggests that how a vacant house is covered can depend on the policy, so do not assume a standard homeowner policy applies. Tell the insurer the real situation: who will live there (if anyone), how many months it will stand empty, and whether you will renovate. Under the duty to disclose and notify, getting this wrong can cause problems at claim time. Ask the insurer to confirm in writing how vacancy is treated.

2. Flood cover is not an all-or-nothing choice

Water-disaster cover (水災補償) is optional in many policies. The hazard map helps, but it is not the same data insurers use. GIROJ itself notes that its classes consider drainage overflow and landslide risk, not only river flooding, so a lot with a modest flood colour on the map can still sit in a higher class. Check the lot on the Hazard Map Portal Site (flood, landslide, storm surge, tsunami layers) and read our walkthrough of how to read the hazard map. Then ask the insurer what the water-disaster cover pays for, and what it would cost to add or drop it. Note that tsunami damage caused by an earthquake falls under earthquake insurance, not water-disaster cover (per the Ministry of Finance page above).

3. Earthquake cover will not rebuild your house

Because the earthquake amount is capped at 30 to 50% of the fire amount, the payout is a fraction of the building value by design. An older wooden house is the kind of building where seismic risk matters most, so ask how the earthquake system grades damage (total, large-half, small-half and partial loss) and what share of the insured amount each grade pays, and consider your own plan for any gap. Can a foreigner buy this insurance?

We found no nationality or visa requirement in the official sources above. In practice, the hurdles are different: Japanese-language application forms, Japanese-language claims handling, and payment methods that assume a Japanese bank account or address. Some insurers and agencies serve overseas owners better than others, and this varies by company, so ask each one directly. If you are still working out whether you can buy at all, start with can foreigners buy an akiya in Japan. Also allow time: your agent and any lender may want proof of cover before settlement.

Costs

We do not quote premium figures here because no official source gives a single price for an old akiya. Premiums depend on building structure, age, location, sum insured, deductible, which perils you include (fire, wind, water disaster, theft and so on), and the policy term. What is documented: the GIROJ water-disaster class system (class 5 is roughly 1.5 times class 1 for that component), the earthquake limits above, and the existence of seismic discounts. Treat any total as a range from at least two or three written quotes, and compare like-for-like cover rather than the cheapest line item. For other purchase-related outlays, see fixed asset tax and acquisition costs.

Checklist: what to ask when requesting a quote

  • Will you insure this building given its age and structure (wood, year built), and will you do so while it is vacant?
  • How is the property classified (occupied home, vacant, second home, rental), and what changes if I move in later?
  • Which perils are included: fire, wind, hail, water disaster, theft, water leakage, and what are the deductibles?
  • What water-disaster class applies to the address, and can I add or remove that cover?
  • What earthquake amount (30-50%) is possible, and does a seismic discount apply to this building?
  • Is there a policy term and a payment option that works for a non-resident, and is there English or translated support for claims?
  • What must I report if use, occupancy, or renovation plans change (duty to notify)?
  • What documents, inspection records or photos do you need before binding cover, and what is the timing relative to settlement?

FAQ

Can a foreigner get fire insurance for a house in Japan?

The official sources cited here set no nationality or residence-status requirement. Underwriting, documents and language support are set by each insurer, so ask directly and early.

Does fire insurance cover earthquake damage in Japan?

No. The Ministry of Finance states that fire insurance does not cover earthquake-caused fire or its spread. Earthquake insurance is a separate add-on, set at 30-50% of the fire amount and capped at 50 million yen (building) and 10 million yen (contents).

Do I need flood (suisai) cover if the hazard map shows a risk?

Treat the map as a starting point. GIROJ's classes include drainage and landslide risk, so they may differ from a flood map. Check the lot, ask what the cover pays for, and decide with the insurer's written terms in hand.
