Akiya well water in Japan: public water, wells, private pipes and the water connection fee (suidō kanyūkin)

Short answer: a rural akiya can be on public water (上水道), a private well (井戸), or a small or shared supply (簡易水道, 組合水道, 私設管). Each has a different cost and risk profile. Public water may need a one-off connection charge (水道加入金) if the house is not yet connected; a well needs water-quality testing; a shared pipe needs written rights over land you do not own. The listing line 水道 (suidō) is often one word covering all three, so confirm which one it is before you make an offer.

How can I tell from a listing which water supply the house has?

Look at the utilities line (設備) of the listing. These are the terms that matter:

Questions to send the agent in Japanese: 上水道・井戸・簡易水道・組合水道のどれですか？ ("Is it public water, a well, a village system, or an association system?") and 水道メーターは設置されていますか？ ("Is a water meter installed?"). Our guide to questions to ask the agent before buying a Japanese house has the wider list.

What is the water connection fee (suidō kanyūkin)?

Suidō kanyūkin (水道加入金) is a one-off charge that many waterworks collect when a property newly joins the public water supply. Depending on the municipality it appears under names such as 給水分担金, 水道利用加入金 or 分担金. It is set by each local government and is normally priced by the diameter of the water meter, so a standard house meter (13 mm or 20 mm) costs far less than a large one. It is separate from the plumber's cost to lay the pipe and install the meter.

The amounts vary widely. These three published examples are illustrations only, not a guide to your municipality; the figures were checked on 2026-10-19 and can be revised:

The spread between 13 mm and 20 mm in these examples shows why the meter size on the application matters. Many waterworks also charge only the difference if you later enlarge the meter (Saitama states this on its page). A rural town may charge less, more, or call it something else, so ask the local waterworks office for the current table. Do not assume the previous owner "already paid": a vacant house may have had its meter removed, and the right to a connection does not always transfer as a prepaid credit. Ask.

Does the water main run in front of the house?

This is the question that turns a small fee into a large bill. The public main (配水管 / 本管) usually runs under the road. From it, a branch pipe (給水管 / 引込管) goes to your meter box. Three situations:

  • Main in the road you front, branch exists: the simple case. Check the meter and the pipe material (very old lead or galvanised pipes may need replacing).
  • Main in the road, no branch yet: you pay for a new branch plus the connection fee. Municipalities publish their own rules for who pays what.
  • The branch crosses a neighbour's land, or the house sits behind another lot: this is the red flag. Pipes that run through land owned by someone else need that owner's consent, and an old verbal arrangement may not bind a new owner. This is the same issue as in our guide to private roads and shared ownership, applied to pipes. Ask for a written agreement or a drawing showing the pipe route.

Pipes owned by an association (組合水道) add a further layer: membership may be tied to the lot, with an entry fee and monthly dues. Get the association's rules, the latest accounts and the name of the officer who handles transfers.

How do I check well water quality (水質検査)?

A well is private infrastructure: the owner is responsible for safety. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's notes on its drinking-well guidelines (飲用井戸等衛生対策要領の留意事項) say owners should test for the items covered by water-quality standards before a well starts supplying water, and that the health centre or municipality should be contacted if contamination is found. Local rules go further. Shizuoka City's drinking-well guidance, for instance, lists bacteria, E. coli, nitrate and nitrite nitrogen, chloride ion, pH, taste, odour, colour and turbidity, and calls for regular testing at least once a year. Whether a well serving only one private home is bound by the same rule depends on the local authority, so ask the local health centre (保健所).

  • Ask the seller for the latest 水質検査結果 and the date.
  • Commission your own test from a registered testing laboratory; the health centre or municipality office can usually name local ones and some run their own testing guidance.
  • Treat the results seriously: bacteria, nitrate and industrial solvents are the typical concerns near farmland or old industry. A failed item may mean disinfection equipment, a filter, a deeper bore, or switching to public water.
  • Check the pump and tank: a pump failure at an old house can cost more than the water test.

What about freezing winters and the septic tank?

In cold regions a vacant house with water left in the pipes can freeze and burst. Ask whether there is a drain-down valve (水抜き栓) or heating cable, how the water was shut off during vacancy, and whether the meter box is insulated. Burst pipes often show up only after the thaw, as water stains or a spinning meter.

Water and drainage are linked. A house on a well is frequently also on a septic tank (浄化槽) or even a vault toilet, because areas without public water often lack public sewers. A bigger consideration if the house is on a septic tank: some municipalities require a connection to the sewer if one is later laid. Read our guide to the jokaso septic tank cost alongside this one, and if the house also uses bottled gas, see propane vs city gas costs. The cheap-house utilities add up together.

Checklist before you make an offer

  • Confirm which supply the house actually uses: public, well, small system, or association.
  • Check whether a meter is installed and whether the house is already connected.
  • Ask the waterworks office whether a connection fee applies, in what amount, and who pays.
  • Confirm the main runs in the road you front; get a drawing of the pipe route.
  • For a shared or crossing pipe, get written permission or association rules before contract.
  • For a well, obtain a recent 水質検査結果 and arrange your own test.
  • In cold areas, ask about freeze protection and winterisation during vacancy.
  • Check sewage: public sewer, septic tank, or vault toilet.

Questions for the municipal waterworks office

  • この住所の前面道路に配水管はありますか？ ("Is there a main in the road in front of this address?")
  • 加入金（分担金）はいくらですか、誰が負担しますか？ ("How much is the connection fee, and who pays?")
  • 過去に給水装置が撤去されていますか？ ("Has the service connection been removed in the past?")
  • 指定給水装置工事事業者を教えてください。 ("Please name designated contractors.")

FAQ

Is well water safe to drink in a Japanese akiya?

It depends on the well. Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare guidelines expect a drinking well to be tested for the items with water-quality standards before it starts supplying water, and municipal guidance such as Shizuoka City's asks for regular testing at least once a year. Ask the seller for a recent test report (水質検査結果) and commission your own test before relying on the water.

What is suidō kanyūkin?

Suidō kanyūkin (水道加入金), also called 給水分担金 or 水道利用加入金, is a one-off charge some waterworks collect when a property newly connects to the public water supply. The amount is set by each municipality, usually by meter diameter, and it is separate from the plumbing contractor's work cost.

How can I tell if an akiya has public water, a well or a private water pipe?

Check the water line of the listing: 公営水道 or 上水道 means public water, 井戸 means a well, and 簡易水道, 組合水道, 私設水道 or 共同水道 mean a small or shared supply. If the listing is silent, ask the agent and confirm with the municipal waterworks office.

Can frozen pipes be a problem in a Japanese akiya?

In cold regions, water pipes in an unheated vacant house can freeze and burst. Ask whether the house has a drain-down valve (水抜き栓) or heating cable, and how the water was shut off during vacancy.
