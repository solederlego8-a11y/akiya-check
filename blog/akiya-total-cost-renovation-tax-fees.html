How much does it really cost to buy an akiya? Purchase price vs renovation, tax and fees

Short answer

The listing price is only part of the cost. Budget separately for fees and taxes, renovation, and running costs before you decide.

The mistake: treating the listing price as the total cost

Akiya listings can look remarkably cheap, and some are genuinely inexpensive. The problem is everything the listing does not show: brokerage and registration costs at purchase, fixed-asset tax every year, and renovation that can cost several times the purchase price. This guide separates the total into three buckets — one-off purchase costs, renovation, and running costs — so you can compare listings on the same basis.

Nothing here is a quote. Costs depend on the property, its location and condition, and the professionals you hire. Build your own estimate from the buckets, then confirm each line with the agent and a judicial scrivener.

Bucket 1: One-off costs at purchase

Fees and taxes around the contract

  • Brokerage fee — if an agent intermediates the sale, a fee applies, calculated under rules set for real-estate transactions.
  • Registration costs — ownership transfer registration involves registration tax plus the fee of the judicial scrivener who prepares it.
  • Stamp duty and contract costs — small but real, and easy to forget.
  • Prorated settlement — fixed-asset tax and similar charges are commonly settled between buyer and seller as of the handover date.

Ask the agent for a written estimate of all closing costs before signing, not after.

Bucket 2: Renovation — the bucket that decides everything

For many akiya, renovation is larger than the purchase price. The range is wide because condition is wide: some houses need cleaning, minor repairs and appliance replacement, while others need roof work, plumbing renewal, bathroom and kitchen replacement, or structural attention. Two points keep estimates honest.

Separate "must fix to live safely" from "want to improve": leaks, drainage, electrical safety and bathing/toilet function come before finishes. And remember that desk-based reports — including our own guide to what an akiya report checks — do not enter the building. Physical condition needs a licensed survey, and renovation quotes need an on-site visit.

Bucket 3: Running costs after you buy

  • Fixed-asset tax and city planning tax — assessed annually on the property; the amount follows the official valuation, not the bargain price you paid.
  • Utilities and maintenance — water, sewer or septic-tank maintenance, electricity, and routine repairs.
  • Insurance, management and exit — fire insurance, management costs if you do not live there full-time, and eventually the cost of selling or demolishing.

Summary

Judge an akiya by the three buckets together: purchase price plus closing costs, must-fix renovation, and running costs. The cheapest listing is often not the cheapest house. Build the estimate line by line, confirm it with local professionals, and only then decide.

FAQ

Q. Is the listing price the total cost of buying an akiya?

No. On top of the price you should expect closing fees and taxes, possible renovation costs that can exceed the price itself, and annual running costs such as fixed-asset tax, utilities and maintenance. Compare listings by adding all three buckets.

Q. How much does renovation of an akiya cost?

There is no reliable single figure: it depends on the building's condition, from cleaning and minor repairs to roof, plumbing and structural work. Get a licensed building condition survey and on-site contractor quotes for the specific house instead of relying on averages.

Q. Can I estimate the total cost from overseas?

Partly. You can collect the price, ask the agent for a written closing-cost estimate, and check official zoning and hazard data remotely. Physical condition and renovation quotes require people on site, so keep those lines as ranges or unknowns until a survey is done.
