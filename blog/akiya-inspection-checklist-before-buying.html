Akiya inspection checklist: what to check before you buy a vacant house in Japan

Short answer

Do not judge an akiya by its purchase price. Walk the property with a fixed checklist — structure, roof, plumbing and septic, boundaries, and legal paperwork — and price the renovation before you sign, not after.

Why a checklist matters more than the listing photos

Most akiya listings show the rooms that photograph well. The costs that decide whether a cheap house is actually cheap sit elsewhere: in the roof, under the floor, in the septic tank, and in paperwork you cannot see in photos. A fixed inspection checklist keeps a short visit from becoming a tour of the nice parts only. Use the same list for every property so you can compare candidates on equal terms.

The checklist, in the order to walk it

1. Structure and roof

  • Roof: visible sagging, slipped tiles or metal sheeting, stains on ceilings below
  • Floors: soft or springy spots, noticeable slope when you walk across a room
  • Posts and beams: cracks, insect damage, water staining at the base
  • Rain gutters and exterior walls: damage that lets water into the structure

2. Water, drainage and septic

  • Is the property on public water and sewer, or on a septic tank (jokaso)?
  • When was the septic tank last inspected or emptied, and is there a record?
  • Do taps run, do drains flow, is there a smell of standing water?
  • Well water, if used: is there a water quality test result?

The septic question alone can change a renovation budget. Our earlier guide on the real total cost of buying an akiya explains how these items add up next to the purchase price.

3. Boundaries, access and the road

  • Can you identify the boundary markers, and do fences match them?
  • Does the plot touch a public road, and is the frontage wide enough for rebuilding rules?
  • Is the access road private, shared, or maintained by the municipality?
  • Can a renovation vehicle actually reach the house?

4. Paperwork that decides what you may do

  • Registry: does the registered owner match the seller, and is the building registered at all?
  • Fixed asset tax records: how is the building recorded and taxed?
  • Rebuilding: is the plot in an area where rebuilding is restricted or not permitted?
  • Vacancy history: how long has the house been empty, and was it heated or ventilated?

What a short visit cannot tell you

A walkthrough checklist catches visible problems. It cannot see inside walls, under a sealed floor, or into the condition of a septic tank that has sat unused. Termite damage, hidden leaks and structural decay are exactly the items that turn a bargain into a loss, and they need a closer inspection with the floor or ceiling opened where permitted. Treat the checklist as the filter that decides which properties deserve that deeper look.

Summary

Check structure and roof first, then water and septic, then boundaries and road access, and finally the paperwork. Price what you find before you buy. A property that survives the checklist is a candidate; one that fails it cheaply has saved you the real cost.

FAQ

Q. Can I inspect an akiya myself with this checklist?

You can cover the visible checkpoints yourself, and the list helps you compare properties. It does not replace a professional inspection: hidden termite damage, leaks inside walls and the real state of an unused septic tank need equipment and access a short visit does not give you.

Q. What is the most commonly missed item?

The septic system and the road. Buyers check rooms and roofs, but an unmaintained septic tank or a plot whose road frontage blocks rebuilding can each cost more than the house, or make the planned renovation impossible.

Q. Should I inspect before or after making an offer?

Walk the property with the checklist before you offer, and arrange the deeper inspection before you sign the purchase contract. Findings are only useful while you can still change the price or walk away.
