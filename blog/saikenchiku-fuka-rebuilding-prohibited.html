Saikenchiku Fuka (再建築不可): Why Some Cheap Houses in Japan Can Never Be Rebuilt — and How to Check Before Buying

Saikenchiku fuka (再建築不可) means the lot does not satisfy Japan's road-frontage rule in Article 43 of the Building Standards Act, so the local building authority will not issue a permit to construct a new building there. The existing house can usually stay standing and be lived in, but if it is torn down, burns down, or collapses, you generally cannot rebuild on the same land without first solving the road-access problem — which can be difficult, slow, or impossible depending on the neighbors and the lot's shape. This is one of the most common reasons a Japanese house is priced far below nearby comparables, and it is rarely spelled out in English.

The road-frontage rule behind saikenchiku fuka

Article 43 of the Building Standards Act (建築基準法) requires that a building site front a road, as defined by the Act, that is at least 4 meters wide, with at least 2 meters of the site's boundary actually touching that road. The official English translation of the article, published by Japan's Ministry of Justice, states the requirement in these terms and sets out a narrow proviso under which a Designated Administrative Agency may approve construction without meeting it — see the Building Standards Act, Article 43, on Japanese Law Translation (the Ministry of Justice's official English-translation portal), or the Japanese original on e-Gov Law Search (e-Gov 法令検索).

The rule exists so that fire trucks and ambulances can reach a building site, and so that people can evacuate it — it dates back to post-war fire-safety planning and applies inside city planning areas and quasi-city planning areas. A lot at the end of a narrow alley, behind another house, or reached only by a footpath under 2 meters wide typically fails this test. Many local governments, such as Nagoya City, publish plain explanations of the related permit process; see Nagoya City's page on Article 43 road-access permits as one example of how a municipality applies the rule in practice.

Why these houses are cheap — and why the discount can be a trap

A saikenchiku fuka lot is functionally a piece of land you can occupy but, in most cases, cannot legally replace the building on. That single fact removes most of the ordinary buyer pool: banks are far more cautious about mortgage lending on land that cannot be rebuilt, developers will not touch it, and many Japanese buyers avoid it outright once they understand the term. Less competition for the lot pushes the asking price down, sometimes to a fraction of a comparable rebuildable property nearby.

The trap is that the discount reflects a real, structural limitation, not just an inefficiency in the market. If the house is old — pre-1981 seismic-standard houses are common in this category — you may be buying a structure with a limited remaining lifespan and a legal ceiling on how far you can go to extend it. Road access is only one of several hidden factors that push a price down; flood, landslide, and tsunami exposure is another, covered in our guide to reading Japan's hazard map before buying an akiya. The cheap price can still make sense for some buyers (for example, someone planning light renovation and long-term personal use), but it is not simply "the same house for less money."

How to spot it in a Japanese listing

Japanese listings rarely say "you cannot rebuild this house" in plain language. Instead, they use a small set of recurring terms, usually in the fine-print or remarks section. Watch for:

再建築不可"Rebuilding prohibited" — stated directly. The clearest possible warning; if you see it, assume no new construction without a fix.
接道義務・接道"Road frontage obligation" — if the listing discusses this at all, the lot's frontage is borderline or in question.
43条但し書き（道路）"Article 43 proviso (road)" — the lot was approved under the narrow exception, not the standard rule. Rebuilding may be possible but requires a fresh application each time, not an automatic right.
セットバック"Setback" — part of the lot must be given up to widen a narrow road to 4 m before building; usable land area is smaller than the registered lot size suggests.
私道"Private road" — the access road is privately owned, possibly by neighbors, which affects both legal access and whether it counts as a Building Standards Act road at all.
敷地延長・旗地（旗竿地）"Flag-shaped lot" — a narrow access strip leading to a wider lot behind it; frontage width is often exactly at or below the legal minimum.
市街化調整区域"Urbanization control area" — a separate restriction that can further limit or block new construction even where road frontage is fine.

None of these terms are automatically fatal, but any one of them means you should ask the agent for the lot's specific frontage width and the road's legal status before making an offer, not after.

What you can — and generally cannot — do after buying

Buying a saikenchiku fuka property does not make the house unusable. In general:

  • Usually allowed: living in the existing structure, interior renovation, repairs, and many forms of remodeling that do not require a new building-confirmation (kenchiku kakunin) application.
  • Often restricted or blocked: full reconstruction after demolition, large-scale extensions that legally count as new construction, and — in many cases — obtaining a standard mortgage, since lenders assess the collateral value of land that cannot be rebuilt very conservatively.
  • Sometimes possible, case by case: qualifying for the Article 43 paragraph 2 proviso, buying or leasing a sliver of neighboring land to create legal frontage, or getting a private road certified as a Building Standards Act road — each of these depends on neighbor cooperation and the local building authority's discretion, and should be confirmed with the municipality's building-guidance section (建築指導課) and a licensed architect or judicial scrivener, not assumed from the listing.

Because so much depends on local, lot-specific facts, this is exactly the kind of item worth verifying before you commit money — either by asking the agent detailed questions in Japanese, or by having the zoning and frontage status checked against official data for that specific lot. A short pre-purchase checklist

  • Does the listing use any of the terms above — 再建築不可, 接道, 43条但し書き, セットバック, 私道, 旗竿地, or 市街化調整区域?
  • What is the exact width, in meters, of the road the lot fronts, and how many meters of the boundary actually touch it?
  • Is that road officially recognized under the Building Standards Act, or is it a private road (私道) with no such status?
  • If a setback is required, how much usable land area remains after it?
  • If the property is marked 43条但し書き, was the current permit granted to the current owner, or would you need to reapply after purchase?
  • Does a lender in Japan (or your home country) treat this lot as mortgageable at all, and on what terms?
  • What is the seismic standard era of the existing structure, and what renovation, rather than reconstruction, would actually be needed?

Frequently asked questions

What does saikenchiku fuka (再建築不可) actually mean?
It means the building lot does not meet Japan's road-frontage rule under Article 43 of the Building Standards Act, so no building permit will be issued to construct a new structure there. The existing house can normally stay standing and be used, but if it is demolished, burns down, or is removed, a new house generally cannot be built on the same footprint without first fixing the road-access problem.

Can a saikenchiku fuka property ever be rebuilt?
Sometimes. Common routes include buying or leasing a strip of neighboring land to create 2 meters of legal frontage, getting the lot recognized under the Article 43 paragraph 2 proviso (often called 43-jo tadashigaki), or having a private road certified as a Building Standards Act road. Each route depends on neighbors' cooperation, the local building authority's discretion, and cost, so it should be confirmed with the municipality and a licensed architect or judicial scrivener before you rely on it.

Is it legal for a foreigner to buy a saikenchiku fuka house in Japan?
Yes. Japan places no nationality or residency restriction on owning land or buildings, including lots that cannot be rebuilt. The restriction is on the lot itself under the Building Standards Act, not on who may own it. The financial risk is that renovation is usually still allowed, while full reconstruction, and often mortgage financing, are much harder to obtain.
