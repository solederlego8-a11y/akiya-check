Buying a house at court auction in Japan (keibai): how the bidding works, and the risks a normal sale doesn't have

Updated 12 November 2026 · Published by the Akiya Check editorial team. General information from public sources, not legal, tax or immigration advice.

Short answer: yes, houses in Japan — including very cheap rural ones — are also sold through court auctions (競売, keibai), run by the district courts under the Civil Execution Act, usually after a mortgage default. Anyone can bid, and there is no agent and no viewing. Instead you judge the property from three court documents, wire a deposit of normally 20% of the court's base price before bidding, and pay the balance in full by a court-set deadline if you win. Prices can be lower than the open market, but the condition and occupancy risks that an agent and a seller would normally absorb all sit with you. 30-second decision

  • You want the lowest possible price and can pay cash, in full, on the court's schedule: auctions are worth watching — the official listings are free on the courts' BIT site.
  • You need a mortgage, an inspection, or a guaranteed vacant house: the auction format fights all three. A normal sale, or an akiya bank, fits better — see how to check a house before buying.
  • You cannot read Japanese legal documents: every auction document is Japanese-only, and the masked public versions hide details you can only read at the court. Budget for professional help before bidding.

Where auction properties are listed: the BIT site and the "3-point set"

All court auction real estate is published on the courts' official BIT (Real Estate Auction Information) site. For each property the court publishes three documents, known as the 3-point set (3点セット):

DocumentWhat it tells you
Property particulars (物件明細書)The court's own statement of the property, including rights a winning bidder would have to take over (for example certain tenancies) and notes on occupancy
Condition report (現況調査報告書)A court enforcement officer's on-site report: differences from the registry, who occupies the property and on what basis, and photographs — often your only look inside
Appraisal report (評価書)The court-appointed appraiser's valuation and the property's regulatory and location details; this valuation becomes the base price

Two cautions. The downloadable versions mask personal information; the unmasked set can be read at the court handling the case. And the officer's report is a snapshot from around the time of seizure — the property's state on the day you take it over can be worse.

How the money works: base price, minimum bid, and the 20% deposit

  • Base price (売却基準価額): set by the court from the appraisal.
  • Minimum effective bid (買受可能価額): the base price minus 20%. Bids below that cannot win, however they are submitted.
  • Deposit (買受申出保証金): to bid you must first provide a deposit of, in the courts' standard wording, normally 20% of the base price — check the exact figure in each property's auction notice, because it can be set higher. It is paid by bank transfer before you submit your bid.
  • Refunds: bidders who do not win (other than the highest and the runner-up, whose deposits are held longer) get the deposit back promptly after the bid opening.

Most sales use period bidding (期間入札): bids are submitted during a set window, then opened on a stated day. The highest bidder at or above the minimum is designated the highest bidder (最高価買受申出人), and the court then decides whether to permit the sale (売却許可決定). If you win, you must pay the balance — your bid minus the deposit — by the deadline the court sets. Miss it, and the sale permission is cancelled, your deposit is not returned, and you are barred from the resale. A mortgage that must complete by a court deadline is difficult to arrange, which is why auction buyers are usually cash buyers.

FAQ

Can a foreigner bid at a Japanese court auction?

The auction rules do not bar bidders by nationality, and buying the property follows the same ownership rules as a normal purchase. In practice everything — documents, notices, deadlines — is in Japanese and handled with the court in Japan, so most overseas bidders work through a representative or professional in Japan.

How much deposit do I need to bid?

Normally 20% of the court's base price (売却基準価額), wired before you bid. The exact amount for each property is printed in its auction notice on the BIT site, and the court can set a higher figure.

Do I get the deposit back if I lose?

Yes. Deposits are returned promptly after the bid opening to everyone except the highest bidder and the runner-up, whose deposits are held until the sale is settled.

Can I look inside the house before bidding?

As a rule, no. There is no viewing system for occupied auction properties. The enforcement officer's condition report, including interior photographs, is the substitute — one reason auction prices discount condition risk.

What if the previous owner or a tenant refuses to leave?

If the property particulars show an occupant without a right that binds you, the buyer can ask the court for a delivery order (引渡命令) after paying. It is a court procedure with its own timeline and fees, so factor it into the price. If a right does bind the buyer, you take the property with that occupant or tenancy in place.

Sources: Courts of Japan, BIT real-estate auction information site (listings, the 3-point set, deposit and bidding rules); BIT, bidding procedure guidance (period bidding, deposit of 20% of the base price, minimum bid at base price minus 20%); Tokyo District Court, procedures for buying real estate at auction. Procedures and deadlines are set per case by the court in charge; confirm in the property's auction notice.
