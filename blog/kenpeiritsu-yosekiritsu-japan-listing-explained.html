Kenpeiritsu and yosekiritsu: the building-coverage and floor-area ratios on a Japanese listing, explained

Updated 9 November 2026 · Published by the Akiya Check editorial team. General information from public sources, not legal, tax or immigration advice.

Short answer: almost every Japanese listing shows two percentages. Kenpeiritsu (建ぺい率), the building-coverage ratio, caps the footprint of the house as a share of the lot. Yosekiritsu (容積率), the floor-area ratio, caps the total floor area of all floors as a share of the lot. Both are set for the lot's use zone in the city plan, under Articles 52 and 53 of the Building Standards Act. They do not restrict the house that already stands there — but they decide what you may extend or rebuild, and a second, quieter rule based on the width of the front road can cut the floor-area limit well below the number printed on the listing. This is general information, not legal or architectural advice.

30-second decision

  • You will keep the house as it stands: note the ratios, but they rarely change your decision. Check instead whether the house is "existing non-conforming" (below) if you might extend later.
  • You plan to extend or rebuild: the ratios are your design brief. Get the designated figures and the front-road width before you buy, and run the worked example below on the actual lot.
  • The lot faces a narrow lane: assume the road rule, not the headline ratio, sets your real floor-area limit — narrow access is common in akiya areas (see also private roads).

On a 200 m² lot with a coverage ratio of 60% and a floor-area ratio of 200%, the footprint may be up to 120 m² and the floors may add up to 400 m² — before the road rule below has its say.

Where the numbers come from

The Act sets a menu of values for each use zone (用途地域), and the municipal city plan picks one value from the menu for each area. Typical menus: low-rise residential zones choose coverage of 30–60% and floor-area ratios of 50–200%; ordinary residential zones choose coverage of 50%, 60% or 80% and floor-area ratios of 100–500%. Rural lots outside the main zoning system can have their own designated figures. The pair that applies to a specific lot appears in the important-matters explanation (see juyo jiko setsumei) and on the municipality's planning map — the listing's numbers should match both, and if they do not, ask why before signing.

The front-road rule that quietly cuts the floor-area ratio

Article 52 adds a second ceiling. If the widest road the lot faces is less than 12 metres, the floor-area ratio is also capped at:

  • Road width (m) × 0.4 in residential zones, or
  • Road width (m) × 0.6 in most other zones (some municipalities designate different factors for some zones).

The smaller of the designated ratio and the road-based figure is your real limit. Niigata City's official guidance works the standard example: designated ratio 200%, front road 4 m in a residential zone → 4 × 0.4 = 160% is the cap. On a 200 m² lot that is the difference between 400 m² and 320 m² of permitted floor area — invisible if you only read the headline percentage.

Relaxations, and the existing-house trap

  • Corner lots: a lot on a corner designated by the local authority can add 10 percentage points to the coverage ratio.
  • Fire zones: a fire-resistant building in a fire-prevention district can also add 10 points; in an 80%-coverage fire-prevention district, a fire-resistant building is effectively outside the coverage limit.
  • Existing non-conforming houses (既存不適格): a house that complied when built but exceeds today's ratios can stay and be used. It is not illegal. But a rebuild must meet today's figures — so the replacement house may have to be smaller than the akiya you are looking at. This is a separate trap from saikenchiku fuka, where rebuilding is barred altogether.
  • Setbacks shrink the lot: where a narrow road is to be widened, part of the lot may be treated as road and excluded from the site area, which lowers both allowances. Road status is covered in private roads and the lot's measured area in boundary surveys.

FAQ

Where do I find a lot's official ratios?

In the use zone (用途地域) set for the lot in the municipal city plan: ask the agent for the zone name and the designated kenpeiritsu and yosekiritsu, and cross-check on the municipality's planning map or at its city-planning counter. The figures also appear in the important-matters explanation before you sign.

The listing says floor-area ratio 200%. Can I always build up to 200%?

No. If the widest road the lot faces is under 12 metres, the floor-area ratio is also capped at the road width in metres times 0.4 in residential zones (0.6 in most others), and the smaller figure wins. A 4-metre road in a residential zone caps you at 160%, whatever the designated ratio says.

The existing house is bigger than today's ratios allow. Is that a problem?

Usually not for keeping and using it: a house that was legal when built does not become illegal (it is treated as existing non-conforming). But a rebuild, and usually a sizeable extension, must fit the current limits — which may mean a smaller house than the one standing there now.

What are typical numbers for a residential lot?

It depends entirely on the zone the city plan picked. Low-rise residential zones use coverage of 30 to 60 percent and floor-area ratios of 50 to 200 percent; ordinary residential zones use coverage of 50, 60 or 80 percent and floor-area ratios of 100 to 500 percent. A pair like 60 percent / 200 percent is common, but only the lot's own designated figures count.

Sources: Building Standards Act (建築基準法), Art. 52 (floor-area ratio, including the front-road rule in para. 2) and Art. 53 (building-coverage ratio, including corner-lot and fire-zone additions); Niigata City, guidance on checks before applying for building confirmation (worked example: designated 200%, 4 m road, cap 160%). Designated figures differ by lot; confirm on the municipal planning map.
