How to check liquefaction risk (液状化) for a Japanese house before buying: the official maps, old river channels and what the agent does not have to tell you

Short answer: liquefaction (液状化, ekijōka) is when loose, water-saturated sand behaves like a liquid during strong shaking, so the ground under a house loses its bearing capacity. The building often survives structurally but ends up tilted, sunk or with broken pipes, and putting it level again is expensive. Japan publishes a national liquefaction tendency map you can check by address in a few minutes, plus older land-history layers that show whether the lot was once a river, a pond or a rice paddy. None of this is in the hazard section an agent must explain before a contract, so you have to check it yourself.

Why liquefaction matters more for cheap houses

Many low-priced akiya stand on exactly the kind of land that liquefies: former rice paddies converted to housing lots, filled-in ponds, old river channels straightened decades ago, reclaimed coast and sandy dune edges. These were cheap to develop and are still cheap to buy. Liquefaction damage was widespread on reclaimed land around Tokyo Bay after the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake and in parts of Ishikawa and Niigata prefectures after the January 2024 Noto Peninsula Earthquake, including inland areas far from the epicentre.

The damage pattern is specific, and it changes your budget rather than your safety in most cases:

  • Tilting and uneven settlement — a house tilted by even a few thousandths causes dizziness and doors that do not close. Re-levelling a wooden house (沈下修正工事) is specialist work typically quoted in the millions of yen.
  • Broken water, sewer and gas lines — underground pipes shear where the ground moves; septic tanks can float upward.
  • Road and lot damage — sand boils, cracked driveways and collapsed garden walls.
  • Resale value — an area known to have liquefied is priced lower afterwards, and re-surveying a lot to prove it is safe is rarely worth it for a cheap house.

What the agent must and need not tell you

Since 28 August 2020, an agent selling a house must show you where the property is on the municipality's flood hazard maps (flooding from rivers, inland water and storm surge) as part of the important matters explanation (重要事項説明). Landslide warning zones and tsunami zones designated under their own laws are also explained. Liquefaction is not on that mandatory list. A conscientious agent may mention a local liquefaction map, and the seller must not hide known past damage, but there is no legal duty to tell you that the lot is on a former river channel.

That gap is why this check belongs in your own due diligence, alongside the flood, landslide and tsunami maps. If the house did tilt or have its foundations repaired after an earthquake, that should appear as a disclosure; see kokuchi jiko (告知事項).

Check 1: The national liquefaction tendency map (5 levels)

The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) and the Geospatial Information Authority (GSI) publish a liquefaction tendency map based on landform classification (地形区分に基づく液状化の発生傾向図). It covers the whole country in a mesh of roughly 250 m × 250 m and shows the relative tendency to liquefy in five levels. It has been available on the national Hazard Map Portal since 17 December 2020 (MLIT press release, Japanese).

  1. Open the Hazard Map Portal and choose 重ねるハザードマップ (overlay hazard map). There is an English interface, but the layer names are clearer in Japanese.
  2. Search the address or move the map to the lot. Use the satellite view to confirm you are on the right building.
  3. In the layer menu, open 土地の特徴・成り立ち (land characteristics and origins) and select 地形区分に基づく液状化の発生傾向図.
  4. Read the colour of the mesh the lot falls in, and look at the neighbouring meshes — a lot on the edge between two levels deserves the higher reading.

How to read it, with the limits the Ministry itself states:

What the map isWhat it is not
A relative ranking of how likely each type of landform is to liquefy, based on where liquefaction happened in past earthquakesA prediction for a specific earthquake, or a statement that a lot will or will not liquefy
Good for screening: comparing two or three candidate houses, or spotting a lot that sits on a former channel or reclaimed landA substitute for a ground investigation at the exact spot. A 250 m mesh can contain both a ridge and a filled pond
Nationwide and consistent, so it works in rural areas with no local mapUp to date with recent ground improvement or fill work on an individual lot

The data behind the layer numbers the levels so that level 1 is the landform most prone to liquefy and level 5 the least; the map legend shows them as colours with short Japanese notes such as 液状化しやすい (liquefies easily) or 液状化しにくい (does not liquefy easily). As a rule of thumb for screening: the two levels at the "liquefies easily" end are a reason to run the next two checks and ask the questions below; the levels at the other end, typically on hill or terrace ground, are usually not where liquefaction risk concentrates, though slope risks then matter more (see our retaining wall and cliff guide).

Check 2: What the land used to be (GSI land-history layers)

The single best clue is the history of the ground. The GSI's free web map, 地理院地図 (GSI Maps), has layers that show it directly:

A house built on a former paddy is extremely common in rural Japan and is not in itself a red flag. A house built on a filled pond, a former river channel or reclaimed land is the combination that most often appears in liquefaction damage reports. Coverage of the classification maps is not complete in every rural area; the aerial photos are available almost everywhere.

While you are on the map, also check MLIT's large-scale fill land map (大規模盛土造成地マップ), which shows housing estates built on large valley fills or side-hill fills. These are a separate earthquake risk (fill sliding), most common in suburban developments from the 1960s–80s.

Check 3: The local liquefaction prediction map

Many prefectures and cities publish their own liquefaction prediction maps (液状化予測図, 液状化危険度マップ, 液状化しやすさマップ) based on borehole data and a specific scenario earthquake. Where they exist, they are more detailed than the national map. Search for the municipality's name plus 液状化 マップ, or look in the municipality's disaster prevention map (防災マップ). MLIT also publishes a guide for municipalities on liquefaction hazard maps, which lists how local maps are meant to be read.

Local maps often use the PL value (液状化指標値): roughly, PL above 15 is described as high risk, 5–15 as moderate, below 5 as low, in the scenario modelled. Check which earthquake the map assumes and when it was made — some predate the 2011 and 2024 experience.

Check 4: Signs on the ground and in the house

If you or someone you trust can visit — or during a video walk-through — look for:

  • A house or garden wall that leans, gaps between the foundation and the ground, or a step that has opened up at the entrance.
  • Doors and sliding panels that drift open or closed by themselves; a marble that rolls on the floor. A digital level app on a phone gives a rough reading; an inspector measures properly.
  • Newer concrete patches on an older foundation, or a driveway relaid in sections.
  • Neighbouring houses that look re-levelled or have newer foundations than their age suggests, and manholes on the road that stand proud of the surface.
  • Ground that is visibly lower than the road, or a lot surrounded by paddies at the same level.

A building inspection (建物状況調査) measures floor and wall inclination and checks the foundation for cracks; see home inspection in Japan. It does not investigate the soil.

Check 5: Ground investigation — when it is worth paying for

A soil test at the lot — in housing usually a screw-weight sounding test (SWS試験, formerly called the Swedish sounding test) — tells you how firm the upper layers are. It is routine before building a new house and is usually inexpensive compared with the house price, but it is rarely done before buying an existing akiya. It makes sense when:

  • you plan to demolish and rebuild (the builder will need the data anyway);
  • the house shows tilt and you need to know whether it is still moving;
  • the purchase price is high enough that a re-levelling bill would change the decision.

For a cheap house you intend to renovate, the map checks above plus a building inspection are usually the proportionate level of diligence. Ask the agent whether the seller has any past ground investigation data (地盤調査報告書) from when the house was built; houses built since about 2000 often have one.

Insurance and liquefaction

Ordinary fire insurance does not cover earthquake damage, including liquefaction. Earthquake insurance (地震保険), which is sold as an add-on to fire insurance, does cover it, and since the 2011 earthquake the damage assessment includes criteria based on how far a wooden house has tilted or sunk because of liquefaction. Payouts are a fixed percentage of the insured amount by damage grade, and the insured amount is capped at a share of the fire insurance amount — so it softens the cost of re-levelling rather than paying for it in full. See fire and earthquake insurance for akiya.

How liquefaction should change your decision

  • Low tendency, high ground, no fill history: no further action for liquefaction; move on to the other checks.
  • Moderate tendency or former paddy: normal for rural Japan. Make sure earthquake insurance is part of the running-cost budget, and check the house for existing tilt.
  • High tendency plus former river channel, filled pond or reclaimed land: price it in. Ask for any construction-era ground data, budget for earthquake insurance, and if you plan to rebuild, assume ground improvement will be needed. If the house already tilts, get an inspector's measurement before you agree a price.

FAQ

Is liquefaction risk shown on the standard Japanese hazard map?

The national Hazard Map Portal has a liquefaction tendency layer, but it is not part of the flood hazard maps that agents must explain before a contract. You have to switch the layer on yourself under 土地の特徴・成り立ち.

Does liquefaction only happen near the sea?

No. Reclaimed coastal land is the best-known case, but liquefaction also occurs inland on former river channels, filled ponds, low-lying paddy land and sandy river plains. The 2024 Noto Peninsula Earthquake caused liquefaction damage well away from the coast in several areas.

Can a liquefaction-prone house still be a good buy?

Yes, if the price reflects it and you plan for it: earthquake insurance, a check for existing tilt, and an understanding that a rebuild would need ground improvement. Many affordable rural houses stand on paddy-derived land.

How accurate is the 5-level liquefaction tendency map?

It is a screening tool. It ranks landform types by their past tendency to liquefy across a 250 m mesh and does not model a specific earthquake or a specific lot. Use it to decide where to look more closely, not as a guarantee either way.

Will Akiya Check tell me if a house is on a former river channel?

The report reads the national liquefaction tendency layer for the lot, including the landform classification it is based on (for example a former river channel or reclaimed land), and whether the lot is on mapped large-scale fill. It does not replace a ground investigation, and items that cannot be determined from official data are marked as unknown rather than guessed.
