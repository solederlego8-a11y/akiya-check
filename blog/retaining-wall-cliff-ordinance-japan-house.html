Retaining walls and the cliff ordinance (がけ条例): checking a sloped Japanese house before you buy

Short answer: if a house stands on or below a slope of more than about 2 metres, two sets of rules can apply. A local cliff ordinance (がけ条例) can require a certified retaining wall or special foundations before you are allowed to rebuild or extend, and an old retaining wall (擁壁) that does not meet today's standards is the owner's responsibility to keep safe — including liability if it collapses onto a neighbour or a road. Old stone walls are common on cheap hillside akiya and the cost of replacing one can exceed the price of the house. You can spot most of this from maps, photos and two questions to the agent before you pay anything.

What counts as a "cliff" (がけ)?

The Building Standards Act requires buildings at risk from slope collapse to have a retaining wall or other safety measures (Art. 19(4)), and lets prefectures and designated cities add detailed rules by ordinance (Art. 40). Those local rules are what people call the cliff ordinance. Each prefecture or city writes its own, but they follow a common pattern:

ElementTypical rule (check the local ordinance)
Definition of a cliffGround sloping at more than 30 degrees with a height of more than 2 m (some areas use 3 m). A slope with a certified retaining wall may still count, depending on the ordinance
Area affectedLand within a horizontal distance of about twice the cliff height from the top or the bottom of the cliff (some areas use 1.5 times)
What is required to build thereA retaining wall that meets current standards, or a building design that is safe without one — for a house above a cliff, foundations taken deep enough; for a house below a cliff, a reinforced concrete wall or structure facing the slope
When it bitesOn new construction, rebuilding and many extensions. A lawful existing house can usually be lived in and repaired as it is

In Tokyo, for example, Article 6 of the Tokyo Metropolitan Building Safety Ordinance (東京都建築安全条例) applies to cliffs over 2 m and the area within twice the cliff height; city pages such as Ota City's guidance on building near cliffs (Japanese) explain the local application. Other prefectures have equivalents with different numbers — search the prefecture's name plus がけ条例 or 建築基準法施行条例 がけ.

Why this matters even if you never plan to rebuild

  • Resale and rebuild value: a lot that can only be rebuilt after building a new retaining wall is worth less to the next buyer, the same way a no-rebuild lot is.
  • Owner's liability: under Article 717 of the Civil Code, the possessor and ultimately the owner of a structure on land is liable for damage caused by a defect in it — and for the owner this liability does not depend on fault. A retaining wall is such a structure. If an old wall on your lot collapses onto the house below or onto a road, you can be liable even though you did not build it.
  • The 2023 embankment law: the Act on the Regulation of Residential Land Development and Specified Embankments (宅地造成及び特定盛土等規制法, commonly 盛土規制法), in force since 26 May 2023, lets prefectures designate wide regulated areas. In those areas, landowners have a duty to keep fill and walls in a safe condition and can be ordered to carry out works (MLIT overview, Japanese).
  • Hazard zones: steep slopes often coincide with landslide warning zones. Inside a special warning zone (土砂災害特別警戒区域, "red zone") new houses must be built to resist the expected force of collapse. See our hazard map guide.

The cost is not theoretical. In a reported Tokyo District Court case (judgment of 18 November 2016), the buyers of a ¥64 million house learned after purchase that the lot fell under the cliff provisions of the Tokyo ordinance and that a protective wall and reinforcement would cost about ¥20.8 million; the court found the brokers liable for failing to explain the restriction. A cheap rural akiya will usually involve smaller numbers, but the ratio of wall cost to house price can be even worse.

Old retaining walls: which ones are a concern

A retaining wall more than 2 m high is itself a structure that needs building confirmation (確認申請) under the Building Standards Act (Art. 88), and walls built in regulated development areas needed permission under the former Residential Land Development Regulation Act. Many old walls predate these rules or were never inspected. Types that municipalities commonly say cannot be assumed safe:

Wall typeJapaneseWhy it is a concern
Dry-stacked or rounded-stone walls空石積み・玉石積みStones held by friction only; no drainage design. Common on rural lots and terraced hillsides
Oya stone walls大谷石積みSoft volcanic tuff blocks that weather and crack; widely used in the Kanto region
Concrete block walls used as retaining wallsブロック積み（コンクリートブロック）Hollow blocks are not designed to hold back soil
Walls extended upward増し積み擁壁A newer layer built on top of an old wall, adding load the base was not designed for
Walls without weep holes, or with bulging or cracks水抜き穴なし・はらみ・クラックWater pressure behind the wall is a leading cause of failure

The strongest evidence that a wall is acceptable is a confirmation certificate and completion inspection certificate (確認済証・検査済証) for the wall, or a development permit record. Ask the agent whether either exists; for walls over 2 m, the municipality's building department may be able to tell you from its records.

How to check a listing for cliff and wall risk

  1. Look at the photos and street view: is the house raised above the road on a wall, or tucked below a bank at the back? Count the courses of stone and estimate the height against a door (about 2 m).
  2. Check the elevation: on GSI Maps (地理院地図), the elevation tool and the slope layer (傾斜量図) show whether the lot sits on or at the foot of a steep slope. A drop of several metres within a few metres of the house is the pattern to look for.
  3. Check the hazard layers: on the Hazard Map Portal, switch on landslide warning zones (土砂災害警戒区域) and steep-slope collapse hazard areas (急傾斜地崩壊危険区域). Also check MLIT's large-scale fill land map — hillside housing estates are often built on fill held by walls.
  4. Read the listing text: words such as がけ条例, 擁壁, 高低差 (difference in level), 法面 (slope face), 傾斜地 (sloping land), 崖地, 造成地 (developed land) and 要セットバック appear in the remarks (備考) or in the legal restrictions section.
  5. Read the important matters explanation: legal restrictions on building (法令に基づく制限) should include the cliff ordinance and any regulated development area. If the statement is silent but the lot is clearly on a slope, ask in writing. See our juyo jiko setsumei guide.
  6. Ask the municipality: the building guidance department (建築指導課) can tell you whether the cliff rule applies and what would be required to rebuild. An agent or a local architect can make the enquiry for you.

Ownership of the wall matters: a wall on the boundary may belong to you, to the neighbour above or below, or be shared, and the owner carries the repair cost and liability. A boundary survey is often what settles it.

What it can cost — and the help available

  • Inspection: a structural engineer or an inspector experienced with walls can give an opinion on an existing wall. A standard building inspection (see home inspection in Japan) looks at the house, not the wall, unless you ask.
  • Repair vs. replacement: clearing weep holes and minor repairs are modest. Rebuilding a wall to current standards involves excavation, reinforced concrete and often access through neighbouring land, and is commonly quoted in the millions of yen for a residential lot.
  • Public works: in designated steep-slope collapse hazard areas, prefectures carry out slope protection works in some cases, usually with a contribution from owners. Ask the prefecture's civil engineering office.
  • Subsidies: several cities subsidise improvement of dangerous cliffs and walls — Yokohama is one example — usually for owners, with conditions. Check the municipality's website before you price the work.
  • Insurance: fire insurance typically covers some landslide damage under its water-damage cover; earthquake-triggered collapse needs earthquake insurance. Neither pays to rebuild a wall for compliance. See fire insurance for akiya.

How a cliff or wall should change your decision

  • Low wall (under 2 m), sound, on flat-ish land: normal; note it and move on.
  • Wall or slope over 2 m, but the house is outside the twice-height zone: lower concern; confirm with the municipality.
  • House within the zone, old stone wall, no certificates: assume you can live in and repair the house but not easily rebuild, and that the wall is your liability. Price it accordingly, and get an opinion on the wall before committing.
  • Red zone, visible bulging or cracks, or a house directly below a high bank: a serious risk for safety as well as money. Many buyers walk away.

FAQ

Does the cliff ordinance stop me from living in the house?

Generally no. The rules mainly apply when you build, rebuild or extend. A lawfully built existing house can usually be occupied and repaired. The effect is on future building options and on value.

Is a retaining wall's height measured from the road or the garden?

It is the vertical difference between the ground at the top and the bottom of the wall or slope. Local ordinances define the measurement points; ask the building department if the lot is borderline around 2 m.

Who is responsible if an old wall collapses onto the neighbour?

Under Article 717 of the Civil Code, liability for a defective structure on land falls first on the person in possession and, if they took necessary care, on the owner — without the owner needing to be at fault. Buying a lot with an unsafe wall means buying that exposure.

Is the cliff ordinance the same everywhere in Japan?

No. Each prefecture or designated city sets its own rule under the Building Standards Act, so heights (2 m or 3 m), distances (1.5 or 2 times) and exceptions differ. Always read the ordinance for the municipality where the house is.

Can I check retaining wall risk without visiting?

Partly. Elevation and slope maps, landslide zones, the fill land map and street view will show whether a wall or cliff is present. The wall's condition and certificates need photos from the agent and a question to the municipality; an engineer's opinion requires a site visit by someone.
