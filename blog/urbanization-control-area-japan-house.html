市街化調整区域 (urbanization control area): can you actually live in, renovate or rebuild a cheap house there?

Short answer: yes, you can live in an existing house in a 市街化調整区域 (shigaika chōsei kuiki, "urbanization control area"). What you often cannot do without a permit is build a new house, rebuild the old one larger or for a different use, or convert it into a shop, guesthouse or rental business. Because permits are uncertain, banks are reluctant to lend against such land, and resale is slower. The price is low for a reason — and the reason is usually manageable if you check it before buying.

What is an urbanization control area?

Under the City Planning Act (都市計画法), each city-planning area can be divided into an urbanization promotion area (市街化区域), where development is encouraged and infrastructure is planned, and an urbanization control area (市街化調整区域), where the policy is to restrain urban sprawl and keep farmland and open land (Art. 7). In a control area:

  • Development (開発行為) — creating a building lot by changing land use or form — requires a permit from the prefecture or designated city (Art. 29), and permits are granted only for listed exceptions (Art. 34).
  • Building on land that was not created by a permitted development also requires a permit (Art. 43), with similar exceptions.
  • Land-use zones (用途地域) are usually not designated, so the familiar residential/commercial zoning does not apply; instead, almost everything needs case-by-case permission.

Source: City Planning Act, Japanese text (e-Gov); MLIT's development permit system overview. Whether a lot is inside a control area is shown on the municipal city-planning map (都市計画図) and in the MLIT Real Estate Information Library layers that Akiya Check reads.

Why are so many akiya in control areas?

Control areas were drawn mostly in the 1970s around existing farm villages. The houses already there were allowed to stay, and their owners aged. Today those villages are exactly where empty houses accumulate: the land cannot be turned into new subdivisions, so there is little developer demand, and heirs living in cities have no use for a house they cannot easily rebuild or sell. Listings at ¥500,000–3,000,000 are common. The house itself may be perfectly sound; the discount reflects the permit regime.

The single most important question is therefore not "can I live here" but "if this house burns down or I want to rebuild, will the prefecture grant a permit, and on what conditions?" The answer depends on the house's original permit status and on local ordinances.

Which houses can be rebuilt? The key concepts

  • Existing lawful building (既存建築物): a house that stood before the area was designated, or was built with a permit afterwards. Rebuilding it for the same use is the most commonly permitted case.
  • Person-specific permits (属人性): many post-designation houses were permitted as farmer's dwellings (農家住宅) or dwellings for a "branch family" (分家住宅). The permit belongs to that person or household; a third-party buyer may not inherit the right to rebuild, and in some prefectures may not even be allowed to occupy it without a separate use-change permit. This is the trap that catches most outside buyers.
  • Art. 34 item 12 / ordinance areas (条例指定区域): prefectures and cities can designate settlements (集落) where building new houses is permitted to anyone by ordinance, with conditions such as a minimum number of existing houses nearby and road access. Inside such an area, an akiya is much closer to a normal lot.
  • Former "existing lot" system (旧既存宅地): until 2001 lots that were residential before designation had a general building right. That system was abolished; some prefectures replaced it with ordinances, others did not. A listing that says 既存宅地 is quoting a status that may no longer carry the rights it used to.

Because these categories are decided prefecture by prefecture, the reliable check is a pre-purchase consultation (事前相談) with the local development-permit desk (開発指導課 / 都市計画課). Agents often do this for serious buyers; you can also ask the seller's agent to obtain a written summary.

Financing and resale: the practical costs

  • Mortgages: most banks decline or restrict loans on control-area land because collateral value is uncertain. Many akiya purchases here are cash. Some regional banks and JA lend if a rebuild permit is confirmed in advance.
  • Land value: the fixed-asset tax assessment is low (good for annual tax), but so is the appraised value for sale. Expect the land component to be a fraction of an equivalent lot in the promotion area.
  • Resale pool: buyers are mainly locals, farmers and cash buyers of second homes. Time on market is long.
  • Business use: converting to a guesthouse (民泊/簡易宿所) or rental usually needs a use-change permit; in many control areas it is not granted. If your plan depends on income from the property, confirm this before buying.
  • Infrastructure: water may be a well or a small local system, sewage a septic tank, and the access road may be a farm road. Each has its own cost line.

How to check a specific listing in 15 minutes

  1. Confirm the designation. Look for 市街化調整区域 in the listing's 都市計画 field, or check the municipal planning map online. Akiya Check reads the MLIT layer for the lot's coordinates.
  2. Ask the agent for the house's permit status: 既存建築物か、開発許可・建築許可を受けた建物か。農家住宅や分家住宅など属人性のある許可ではないか。
  3. Ask whether the lot is inside an ordinance-designated settlement area: 条例指定区域（法34条12号）に含まれていますか。
  4. Ask whether same-scale rebuilding would be permitted: 同規模・同用途で建て替える場合、許可の見込みはありますか。
  5. If you plan any business or rental use, ask directly whether a use change is possible. Expect "no" and be pleasantly surprised.
  6. Ask the agent to obtain a note from the permit desk (事前相談の回答) before you pay a deposit.

Legal restrictions are among the items an agent must explain in the 重要事項説明 (explanation of important matters), so a good agent will have this information ready. If the explanation only says "市街化調整区域（建築には許可が必要）" with no detail, push for the specifics above.

FAQ

Can a foreigner buy a house in an urbanization control area?

Yes. Ownership rules do not depend on nationality or residence status (see our guide). The permit regime applies equally to everyone; the person-specific permits (farmer's dwelling etc.) can be a problem for any non-farming buyer, Japanese or not.

If I can never rebuild, is the house worthless?

Not worthless, but its value is tied to the standing structure. Maintain it well, insure it, and accept that the exit is slower. Some buyers deliberately choose such houses for the low price and plan to hold long-term.

Is 市街化調整区域 the same as 再建築不可?

No. 再建築不可 usually refers to a lot that fails the road-frontage rule of the Building Standards Act, where rebuilding is prohibited outright. In a control area, rebuilding is permit-dependent, and same-use reconstruction of a lawful house is often allowed. A lot can suffer from both problems at once; see the saikenchiku fuka guide.

Can I farm the land or keep it as a garden?

Using land for agriculture or as a garden is not "development" and needs no City Planning Act permit. Buying farmland (農地) itself is a separate matter under the Agricultural Land Act and usually requires approval from the agricultural committee, which is difficult for non-farmers; a house lot (宅地) with a vegetable patch is fine.

Does the control-area status ever change?

Municipalities review the division periodically and occasionally move land into the promotion area when infrastructure is built, or designate ordinance areas that relax building rules. It does happen, but it should not be part of a purchase plan.

Sources: City Planning Act (都市計画法) Art. 7, 29, 34, 43; MLIT development permit guidance; prefectural development-permit handbooks (e.g. 開発許可制度の手引き, which set the local conditions for reconstruction and ordinance areas). Rules on person-specific permits and rebuild thresholds vary by prefecture and city — confirm with the local permit desk for the specific lot.
