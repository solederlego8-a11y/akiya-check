Does buying a house in Japan give you a visa? No: what owning an akiya changes and what it does not

Updated 13 October 2026 · Published by the Akiya Check editorial team. General information from public sources, not legal, tax or immigration advice.

Short answer: no. Owning a house in Japan does not by itself give you a visa or a status of residence. In Japan, a status of residence is granted for a defined activity or family status, such as working under a particular category, studying, joining a spouse, or managing a business, not for holding real estate. Foreigners are generally free to buy property in Japan whatever their visa situation (see can foreigners buy akiya), but the house will not extend your stay. If you are thinking "I will buy a cheap akiya and then be able to live there", fix the immigration question first and the property question second. This guide is general information and not immigration advice; the Immigration Services Agency and a licensed professional decide individual cases.

30-second decision

  • You are on a visa-free short stay and hope the house lets you stay longer: it does not. Ownership gives no right to stay beyond the permitted period.
  • You want to live in the house long term: work out which status of residence fits your real activity first, then buy.
  • You only want a holiday home or a place to visit a few times a year: buying is possible, but plan your stays within the limits of your entry conditions and budget for the empty-house costs.

What the Immigration Services Agency grants status for

The Immigration Services Agency (ISA, part of the Ministry of Justice) publishes the list of statuses of residence and the conditions for each at its status pages. The categories are built around what you do in Japan or your family relationship, for example working in a specialist field, studying, being the spouse or child of a Japanese national or resident, or managing a business. None of the categories on those pages is described as "owning real estate".

The ISA page for the Business Manager status is a useful example, because people often ask about it. It describes the status as covering the activity of running a trade or other business in Japan or managing such a business. The documents listed there include proof that you have business premises (such as a registry certificate and a lease agreement for the office), proof of at least one full-time employee, proof of Japanese ability, a business plan and financial statements. In other words, real estate appears in that list only as evidence of an office for a real operating business, not as a qualification. The detailed requirements, including the capital level, have been revised in recent years, so read the current ISA page and the guidelines it links to, rather than relying on a blog post, including this one.

What a visa-free short stay allows

Citizens of many countries can enter Japan for a short stay without applying for a visa first. The Ministry of Foreign Affairs explains the visa system, including the exemption arrangements and the list of countries, on its visa information pages. Two points matter for a house buyer:

  • The permitted period depends on your nationality and the arrangement that applies to you. Check the period for your passport on the MOFA page before you plan your trips.
  • A short stay is for purposes such as tourism, visiting relatives or short business contacts. It is not for working. Running a rental or renovating the house yourself to sell it is a different question from simply visiting it; if you plan activities that look like work, ask the ISA before you travel.

Owning a house does not change either point. A house is not a reason for the immigration officer to extend a stay, and there is no ownership-based exception to the rules above that this guide can point you to. If a seller, a broker or a website tells you otherwise, ask for the exact ISA page that says so.

Other routes that exist, and why this guide does not rank them

The ISA's Designated Activities page lists some long-stay arrangements, for example for long-term stays for tourism or leisure and for remote workers ("digital nomads"), alongside many unrelated designations (ISA: Designated Activities). These arrangements have their own conditions, such as nationality lists and financial conditions, and they change. We have not tested any of them and we do not know whether you qualify. They are relevant to this guide only because they show that Japan's long-stay options are decided by those conditions, not by whether you own a house.

For permanent residents, the situation is different in one way that matters for buying: some home loans are open only to Japanese nationals and permanent residents. See our guide to mortgages and cash purchases for foreigners.

FAQ

Can I get a long-term visa if I buy an akiya?

Not because of the purchase. The ISA's pages describe statuses by activity or family relationship; real estate is not a qualifying ground on those pages. Ask the ISA or a licensed professional about the status that fits what you actually plan to do.

Does owning property let me stay longer than the visa-free period?

No ownership-based extension is described on the MOFA or ISA pages we cite. The permitted period depends on your entry conditions, and you should check it for your nationality before you travel.

Can I still buy a house if I do not live in Japan?

Generally yes. Residence is not a requirement to buy. See can foreigners buy akiya and the remote purchase guide for the practical steps.

Where do I ask about my own case?

The ISA's Immigration Information Center is listed on the ISA pages (0570-013904 at the time of writing). For tax or property questions, ask a licensed professional. We cannot give individual immigration advice.
