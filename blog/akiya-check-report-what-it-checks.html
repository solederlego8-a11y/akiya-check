What a Japanese akiya inspection report actually checks

Short answer: an Akiya Check report checks the listing and the lot, not the inside of the house. It reads the Japanese listing text for risk facts, checks the exact coordinates of the lot against official zoning, hazard and transaction datasets, and marks every item OK, CAUTION, RISK or UNKNOWN. That is a different document from a Japanese home inspection report, which is a physical survey of the building. This guide lists the report's checks one by one, explains the marks and the A–E grade, and states plainly what the report does not check.

First, two reports that are easy to confuse

In Japan, two different things get called an "inspection report":

  • Building condition survey (既存住宅状況調査) — a licensed technician physically visits the house and visually checks two areas only: the main structural parts and the parts that keep rain out. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) publishes the system behind it, including the technician training scheme, on its existing-housing market pages. Our home inspection guide explains its scope, the seller-consent requirement, and what it leaves out.
  • Akiya Check risk report — no visit. It is built from the listing text itself plus official datasets (zoning, hazards, population, transactions), and it answers a different question: "what could make this cheap house a bad purchase, before anyone travels to see it?"

Neither replaces the other. The risk report is the filter you run first, from overseas; the physical survey is what you arrange later, with the seller's consent, for a house that survives the filter.

The six blocks the report checks

1. Can you rebuild?

  • Rebuilding prohibited (再建築不可, saikenchiku-fuka)
  • Road frontage (接道) and setback requirements
  • Urbanization control area (市街化調整区域)
  • Land-use zone, building-coverage ratio and floor-area ratio (用途地域・建蔽率・容積率)

2. Costs

  • Listing price
  • One-off acquisition costs (given as a range)
  • Fixed-asset tax (given as a range)
  • Pre-1981 seismic standard flag — whether the house was built under the old earthquake standard

3. Natural hazards

  • Maximum-scale flood depth
  • Tsunami inundation
  • Landslide warning zones
  • Liquefaction tendency
  • Designated disaster hazard districts

These are read from MLIT's Real Estate Information Library datasets at the lot's exact coordinates — the same official data behind our hazard map guide.

4. Location and exit

  • Population trend in the 250 m mesh around the lot
  • Recorded nearby transactions, so you can sanity-check the asking price
  • The geocoded position, so you can verify the location yourself on a map

5. Utilities and tenure

  • Public water vs. well water
  • Public sewer vs. septic tank (浄化槽)
  • City gas vs. propane (LPG)
  • Freehold vs. leasehold (借地権, shakuchiken)
  • Disclosure matters (告知事項, kokuchi jiko) stated in the listing
  • Akiya-bank conditions attached to the listing (residency, renovation duties and similar)

6. Questions for the agent

Every item the listing leaves unclear becomes a question you can send to the agent as-is, in English and Japanese. This block is generated from the UNKNOWN marks — see below.

How to read the marks, the score and the grade

  • OK / CAUTION / RISK — the check found the fact, and this is how it reads for a buyer.
  • UNKNOWN — the listing did not say. Unknowns stay unknown: the report never guesses, and each one tells you what to ask.
  • Risk score 0–100 — higher means more risk. Grade A (safest) to E (highest risk) — deal-breakers are shown first, before costs and details.

What this report does not check

The report does not enter the building, so it says nothing about physical condition: cracks, tilt, rot, leaks, termites, or the state of plumbing and wiring. Even the official physical survey is narrower than most buyers expect — MLIT's inspection guideline illustrations describe a non-destructive, visual survey of structural and rain-intrusion parts, which does not judge seismic performance or the cause of a defect. If physical condition is your deciding factor, budget for the licensed survey as well; our 10-step due-diligence guide shows where each check fits in the buying order.

FAQ

Q. Is an Akiya Check report the same as a home inspection in Japan?
A. No. A Japanese home inspection (既存住宅状況調査) is a physical, mostly visual survey of the building itself by a licensed technician, limited to structural parts and rain-intrusion points. An Akiya Check report never visits the house: it reads the listing text and checks the lot against official zoning, hazard and transaction data, then scores the risks. The two answer different questions and are often used together.

Q. What does UNKNOWN mean in the report?
A. It means the listing did not state the fact, so we do not guess. Every UNKNOWN item is turned into a ready-to-send question for the agent, in English and Japanese, so you can ask before you commit.

Q. How are the risk score and grade decided?
A. Each checked item is marked OK, CAUTION, RISK or UNKNOWN, deal-breakers first. The marks are combined into a risk score from 0 to 100 — higher means more risk — and a grade from A (safest) to E (highest risk). The report shows the item-by-item marks behind the score, so the grade is never a black box.

Q. Can the report tell me whether the house is structurally sound?
A. No. The report is built from the listing and official datasets, not from entering the building. Structural condition, leaks and other physical defects are the job of a licensed building condition survey, which has its own limited scope.
