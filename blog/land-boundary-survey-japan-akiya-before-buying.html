Land boundary survey before buying a house in Japan: 境界未確定, registered vs. surveyed area, and who pays to fix it

Short answer: in Japan the line between your land and the neighbour's is often not where the fence is, and the land area on the listing is often not what is on the ground. On cheap rural houses it is common to see 境界未確定 (boundaries not confirmed) and 公簿売買 (sale based on the registered area) in the contract. Neither stops the sale, but they move the cost and trouble of settling the boundaries from the seller to you. A full boundary-confirmation survey (確定測量) with every neighbour's signature typically costs hundreds of thousands of yen and takes months, so decide before the contract whether the seller does it, you do it, or you accept the risk at a lower price.

Why boundaries are often unclear in Japan

Japan's land records were built from late-19th-century tax surveys. Many parcels have never been precisely measured since. The government's modern cadastral survey programme (地籍調査), which re-measures parcels and produces accurate maps, has covered only around half of the country's surveyable land nationally according to MLIT, with large differences between prefectures and lower coverage in many cities and mountain areas (MLIT cadastral survey site, Japanese). The result, on an old akiya:

  • the official map at the legal affairs bureau may be an old 公図 that shows shape and neighbours roughly but not accurate distances;
  • the registered area (登記簿面積, 公簿面積) may be larger or smaller than the real area — old rural records were often understated;
  • boundary markers (境界標) may be missing, buried, or moved during road works;
  • neighbours, often elderly or themselves absentee owners, may not agree where the line is.

The registry, maps and survey plans can be obtained from any legal affairs bureau or online through the registration information service. Ask the agent for the boundary confirmation documents; if they say 境界確認書なし (none), you know where you stand.

Three kinds of "boundary" — and why the difference matters

Japanese practice separates:

  • 筆界 (hikkai, parcel boundary): the line fixed when the parcel was first registered. Under the Real Property Registration Act it cannot be moved by agreement between neighbours; it can only be found.
  • 所有権界 (ownership boundary): the line up to which each person owns land. Neighbours can agree to move this by a transfer of part of a parcel — but then the parcel has to be subdivided and registered.
  • 占有界 (the line of actual use): where fences, walls, hedges and gardens actually are.

On an old house the three can differ. A wall that was built 30 cm onto the neighbour's parcel decades ago does not move the parcel boundary, and in some circumstances long possession can give rise to acquisitive prescription claims. These disputes are rare on a quiet rural lot but expensive when they happen, and they block a later sale.

Registered-area sale vs. surveyed-area sale

Contract typeJapaneseWhat it means for you
Sale by registered area公簿売買The price is fixed on the registered area. If a later survey shows less land, there is no price adjustment. Normal for cheap rural houses and large irregular lots
Sale by surveyed area実測売買A survey is done (usually by the seller before settlement) and the price is adjusted per square metre for any difference. Normal for urban lots where land is the main value

For a ¥3 million house on a large rural lot, a 公簿売買 with an unconfirmed boundary is often acceptable — the land is not where the value is. It becomes a problem when the lot is narrow, when the road frontage is close to the 2-metre minimum needed to rebuild (see saikenchiku fuka), when you plan to extend or build a new house near the edge, or when a neighbour already disputes the line.

What a boundary survey involves, costs and takes

Boundary work is done by a land and house investigator (土地家屋調査士), a licensed profession that handles surveys and the registration of land and buildings. Their national federation has an English introduction. There are two levels of work:

SurveyJapaneseWhat you getTypical scale
As-is survey現況測量A measured drawing of the lot as it is, based on existing markers and fences. No neighbour agreementDays to a few weeks; usually the cheaper option
Boundary-confirmation survey確定測量Every boundary confirmed on site with each neighbour (and the road authority where the lot touches a public road), markers set, confirmation documents signed. Can be followed by an area correction in the registryCommonly one to several months; longer if a public road or river is involved or a neighbour cannot be reached. Price lists from surveyors commonly run to several hundred thousand yen, more where the authorities must attend

Costs depend on the number of neighbours, the lot shape, whether the road boundary needs confirming and whether old records exist. Ask for a written estimate (見積書) before you agree who pays.

If a neighbour refuses or cannot be traced, the next step is the parcel boundary determination system (筆界特定制度) at the legal affairs bureau, created in 2006: an official, after investigation and hearing an outside expert, states where the original parcel boundary is (Ministry of Justice, Japanese). It is cheaper and faster than a lawsuit but still takes many months, and it is the registered owner who applies — so it is a tool for after you buy, not before.

Neighbour rules that changed in 2023

Amendments to the Civil Code that took effect on 1 April 2023 help owners of old houses with absent or unresponsive neighbours:

  • Overhanging branches (Art. 233): if a neighbour's branches cross the boundary and the neighbour does not cut them within a reasonable time after being asked, or cannot be found, you may cut them yourself.
  • Using the neighbour's land for works (Art. 209): you have a right to use adjoining land to the extent necessary to build, repair or survey near the boundary, with notice.
  • Utility lines (Art. 213-2): you may run water, sewer, gas and electricity lines through or connect to facilities on neighbouring land where there is no other way, with notice and compensation.

These make an unconfirmed boundary easier to live with; they do not confirm the boundary.

When an unconfirmed boundary should change your decision

  • Large rural lot, house well inside it, no disputes: usually acceptable with a 公簿売買; walk the markers you can find and photograph them.
  • Narrow lot or road frontage close to 2 m: ask for at least the road boundary to be confirmed before contract. A few tens of centimetres decide whether you can rebuild.
  • You plan to subdivide, sell part, or separate the house from adjoining farmland (see akiya with farmland): a confirmed survey will be needed anyway; negotiate who pays now.
  • Existing dispute or a wall clearly on the wrong side: price it heavily or walk away — it will follow you to resale.

FAQ

Is it normal to buy a house in Japan without a boundary survey?

For cheap rural houses, yes. Many are sold on the registered area with no survey and boundaries described as not confirmed. It is a risk you take on, and it should be reflected in the price.

Who pays for the boundary survey, buyer or seller?

There is no fixed rule. In urban sales the seller commonly provides a confirmed survey; in low-priced akiya sales the seller often refuses. Whatever is agreed must be written into the contract.

The land is smaller than the listing says. Can I get money back?

Not if the contract is a 公簿売買 that excludes price adjustment — that is exactly what that wording means. If the contract is a 実測売買 or has an adjustment clause, the difference is settled at the agreed rate per square metre.

Can neighbours agree to move the boundary?

They can agree to transfer land between them, which moves the ownership line, but the original parcel boundary (筆界) cannot be changed by agreement. The transfer requires a subdivision and registration done by professionals.

How long does a boundary confirmation survey take?

Commonly one to several months. It takes longer when a public road or river is involved, when there are many neighbours, or when a neighbour is absent or has died without the inheritance being registered (see inheritance registration).
