Home Inspection in Japan for an Akiya: How It Works, What It Covers, and What to Budget (2026)

A home inspection in Japan (建物状況調査, tatemono jokyo chosa, also called 既存住宅状況調査) is a mostly visual, non-destructive check of a used house by a licensed architect who has completed a government-registered training course. It covers two areas only: the main structural parts and the parts that keep rain out. It does not judge earthquake resistance, and it needs the seller's consent. If you are eyeing an akiya (空き家, an empty house) or any other cheap house in Japan, the inspection is one of the few ways to learn about the building itself before you pay. We could not confirm a fee schedule on official pages, so this guide does not quote a price. It shows how to get written quotes instead.

How the inspection system works in Japan

Japan's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) explains on its building condition survey (inspection) page that the survey follows the national Existing Housing Condition Survey Method Standards (既存住宅状況調査方法基準, Ministry Notification No. 82 of 2017) and is carried out by an existing housing condition survey technician (既存住宅状況調査技術者). In practice that means an architect (建築士) who has completed a training course run by an institution registered with MLIT.

The same MLIT page describes what changed in the April 2018 amendment to the Real Estate Brokerage Act (宅地建物取引業法):

  • At the brokerage contract stage, the agent must state in writing whether it will arrange (あっせん) an inspector for you.
  • In the Important Matters Explanation (重要事項説明), the agent must explain whether an inspection was done and what it found.
  • In the sales contract document (37条書面), the inspection result must be recorded where relevant.

Note what this does not do. The law requires the agent to say whether it offers to arrange an inspection. It does not require an inspection to happen, and an agent may answer that it does not arrange them. In that case you can look for a registered inspector yourself. The association that supports the related defect-insurance system, the Japan Housing Defect Warranty Liability Insurance Association (住宅瑕疵担保責任保険協会), lists a dedicated section on 既存住宅状況調査 and links to a search site for technicians.

What the official inspection covers, and what it leaves out

According to MLIT's inspection guideline illustrations (PDF), the survey is limited to:

  • Structural parts (構造耐力上主要な部分): foundations, walls, columns, floors, roof framing and similar elements, checked for signs of deterioration such as cracks, tilt, or rot.
  • Rain-intrusion parts (雨水の浸入を防止する部分): roof, exterior walls, and openings, checked for signs of leaks or water damage.

The method is a "non-destructive survey centered on visual inspection" (目視中心の非破壊調査). Under-floor and attic spaces are viewed through access hatches (点検口). The same document states that the survey does not determine:

  • the seismic performance of the house (耐震性),
  • whether the building violates building codes, or
  • the cause of a defect, meaning whether it is a structural flaw or ordinary wear.

Because the official scope is limited to those two areas, other things you might assume are included may not be. Termite damage, the detailed condition of plumbing, water heaters, wiring and the septic tank, and anything behind a hatch that cannot be opened or entered are not listed in that scope. Some inspection companies sell these as optional add-ons, so do not assume. Ask each company to state in writing what it will and will not look at. Inspection vs. seismic diagnosis vs. defect insurance

These three are often confused:

  • Home inspection (建物状況調査): a visual check for deterioration in the structure and rain-proofing. It does not rate earthquake performance.
  • Seismic diagnosis (耐震診断): a separate assessment of earthquake resistance, especially relevant for houses built under the old standard. Our guide to the pre-1981 seismic standard explains why the construction year matters.
  • Existing-home sales defect insurance (既存住宅売買瑕疵保険): insurance covering structural defects and water intrusion after the sale. MLIT's defect-insurance inspection page says the system is meant to be used alongside building condition surveys, and MLIT's existing-housing market page describes it as a scheme that pairs an expert building inspection with a warranty.

The practical point: an insurance inspection is its own procedure with its own conditions, and a house may or may not qualify. If you want insurance, ask the agent early whether the house could be insured and which registered business would handle it. Do not assume the inspection alone gives you coverage.

Three things buyers miss about akiya inspections

1. The seller must agree

The inspector has to enter the house and open hatches. The guideline document treats the seller's consent as a precondition. For an akiya, the "seller" may be one of several heirs or a family member abroad, so consent can take time. If ownership is unclear, read our note on inheritance registration for an akiya first.

2. A report is a snapshot of visible conditions, not a verdict

The inspection lists deterioration events it can see. It does not say what to fix first, what it will cost, or whether the house is legally rebuildable. Rebuild rights, zoning and hazard exposure sit outside it. Those are separate checks; see saikenchiku fuka (rebuilding prohibited) and urbanization control areas.

3. Empty houses hide access problems

A house that has been empty for years may have stuck hatches, stored belongings blocking the floor, or no power or water. Where the inspector cannot reach an area, the report can only say it was not inspected. Ask the agent to have the seller clear access and turn on utilities before the visit.

Building inspection cost in Japan: what to budget

The official pages we checked (MLIT and the defect-insurance association) do not publish a standard fee, and we found no government fee schedule. Inspection companies set their own prices, so we do not give a number. Treat any figure you see in a blog as an unverified guess, including ones on other sites.

What you can do is compare like for like. Ask two or three registered inspectors for a written quote and confirm:

  • the house type, size and location the quote assumes, including any travel charge for a remote site;
  • whether under-floor and attic inspection through hatches is included;
  • whether termite checks or equipment checks are optional add-ons and what they cost;
  • the report format, photo count, and whether an English summary or translation is offered;
  • whether there is an extra charge for a separate insurance inspection.

Put the quote next to the other purchase costs. Our guide to fixed asset tax and acquisition costs lists the fees that usually get forgotten.

How to get a house inspected in Japan as a foreigner

Nothing in the system requires you to be Japanese or to live in Japan, but the process runs in Japanese and depends on the seller's cooperation. A workable order for a remote buyer:

  1. Ask the agent about the あっせん. Ask whether the agent offers to arrange an inspector, and get the answer in writing. If the answer is no, ask permission to bring your own.
  2. Find a registered inspector. Look for a registered architect who completed the technician course. The insurance association's site links to a technician search.
  3. Get permission before the offer, not after. Ask the agent to confirm the seller accepts an inspection, the dates, and who will open the property.
  4. Ask for a photo-heavy report. Request photographs with each finding and a written explanation you can run through translation, or ask for an English summary if offered.
  5. Read it against the listing. Compare the findings with the listing and the construction year. Our akiya listing red-flags guide helps with the wording.
  6. Raise findings before you sign. Bring them to the agent while the Important Matters Explanation is still being prepared.

If you are still working out the overall order of checks, start with how to check a house in Japan before buying. An inspection belongs near the end, after the free desk checks have not ruled the property out.

A short inspection checklist

  • Asked the agent in writing whether it arranges an inspector (あっせん)
  • Seller consent and access dates confirmed before making an offer
  • Inspector confirmed as an architect who completed the technician course
  • Written quote received, with inclusions and exclusions listed
  • Access to under-floor and attic hatches cleared, utilities on where possible
  • Report includes photos and an English summary or usable text for translation
  • Seismic diagnosis and defect insurance asked about separately
  • Hazard, zoning and rebuild-rights checks done independently of the report

FAQ

Can a foreigner get a home inspection in Japan for a house they do not own yet? Usually yes, but only with the seller's consent, because the inspector must enter the house and open access hatches. Ask the agent early, ideally before you make an offer, and ask whether the inspector may attend without you present. Confirm details with the agent and the inspection company.

How much does a building inspection cost in Japan? We could not confirm a fee schedule on the official pages we checked, so we do not quote a figure. Fees are set by each company and depend on the house, the location and the report options. Get written quotes from two or three registered inspectors and compare what each includes.

Does a home inspection replace a seismic diagnosis or defect insurance? No. The official inspection looks for visible deterioration in the structure and rain-proofing and does not judge seismic performance. A seismic diagnosis is a separate assessment, and existing-home defect insurance is a separate product with its own inspection and conditions.
