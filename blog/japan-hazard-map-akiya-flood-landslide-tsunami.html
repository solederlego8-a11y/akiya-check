How to Read Japan's Hazard Map Before Buying an Akiya: Flood, Landslide & Tsunami Zones Explained

Direct answer: Before you make an offer on any Japanese property, look it up on two free government tools: the Hazard Map Portal Site (ハザードマップポータルサイト) run by the Geospatial Information Authority of Japan, and the Real Estate Information Library run by the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT). Together they show expected flood depth, landslide warning zones, and tsunami inundation for the exact address — information that is legally required to be shown to you for flood risk only, and often left out of the listing for everything else.

Where to check: the two official tools

The primary tool is disaportal.gsi.go.jp, which hosts the "重ねるハザードマップ" (overlay hazard map). It lets you stack flood/inundation (洪水・内水), landslide/sediment disaster (土砂災害), storm surge (高潮), and tsunami (津波) layers on one map, plus municipal-level "わがまちハザードマップ" links. The interface is Japanese-only, but you do not need to read Japanese to use it:

  1. Open the site and use the search box to enter the address (a romanized address usually works, or paste the postal code) or search by place name in Japanese if you have it from the listing.
  2. Turn on one hazard layer at a time from the menu — flood, landslide, then tsunami — rather than all at once, so the color overlay for each is clear.
  3. Zoom in until the map centers on the parcel, and read the color at that exact point, not just the general area, since risk can change block by block.
  4. Use a browser translation extension (e.g., Chrome's built-in translator) to translate menu labels and legend text if needed, but confirm colors against the original Japanese legend since translated legends can shift shading.

The second tool, reinfolib.mlit.go.jp (不動産情報ライブラリ), is MLIT's property-data platform. It has an English-language option and covers transaction price history, urban planning designation, and other disaster-prevention reference data by address, which is useful for cross-checking zoning alongside the hazard layers above. It complements, but does not replace, the hazard map portal's flood, landslide, and tsunami overlays.

Understanding flood depth ranks (想定最大規模)

The flood layer on disaportal.gsi.go.jp is based on the "assumed maximum scale" (想定最大規模) rainfall scenario — the largest flood a river is modeled to produce, not an average-year event. Depths are shown in four color bands, and what each one means for daily life is roughly:

  • Under 0.5m: Water may reach the floor level of a typical ground floor. Vehicles and low furniture are at risk; the structure itself is usually not.
  • 0.5m–3m: Water can reach above adult waist height and into a single-story home's living space; a one-story house could be fully submerged at the upper end of this band.
  • 3m–5m: Water can reach the second floor of a typical two-story house, meaning vertical evacuation within the building may not be safe.
  • 5m or more: Water can exceed the roofline of a two-story house; this band generally corresponds to areas near major rivers or in low-lying basins.

These bands describe an assumed worst-case event over the river's watershed, not a certainty for any given year — but they are the same data set that a licensed agent is required to show you (see below), so it is worth reading it yourself before that meeting rather than relying on a verbal summary.

Landslide warning zones: yellow zone vs. red zone

Under Japan's Act on Sediment-Related Disaster Countermeasures (土砂災害防止法), municipalities designate two tiers of landslide-prone land, both visible on the same hazard map portal's 土砂災害 layer:

  • 土砂災害警戒区域 (Sediment Disaster Warning Area, "yellow zone"): Land where a landslide, debris flow, or slope failure could endanger residents' lives or bodies. Designation mainly triggers hazard awareness and evacuation-planning duties for the municipality.
  • 土砂災害特別警戒区域 (Sediment Disaster Special Warning Area, "red zone"): A subset of yellow-zone land where a landslide could damage or destroy a building and seriously injure occupants. Red-zone designation adds legal restrictions on certain development and can require reinforced structural specifications for rooms used as living space.

Many akiya sit on hillside or valley-edge lots precisely because that land was cheap to build on decades ago — which is also why it is disproportionately likely to fall inside one of these zones today. The zone status is not always visible from the road, and older listings rarely mention it, so this is a layer worth checking even when a property looks flat and unremarkable in photos.

Tsunami inundation zones (津波浸水想定)

For any property near a coastline, the same hazard map portal has a separate 津波 (tsunami) layer, also based on an assumed maximum-scale scenario set by each prefecture. Like the flood layer, it is shown as color-coded depth bands over the modeled inundation area. Coastal akiya — including many in scenic fishing-village locations popular with overseas buyers — can carry meaningful tsunami exposure even where flood-river risk is low, so check this layer independently rather than assuming "not on a river" means "no water risk."

The 2020 disclosure rule — and its limit

Since August 28, 2020, Japan's Building Lots and Buildings Transaction Business Act enforcement regulations (宅地建物取引業法施行規則) have required a licensed agent to show the buyer or tenant the property's approximate location on the local flood hazard map as part of the mandatory Important Matters Explanation (重要事項説明) — announced by MLIT on July 17, 2020. In practice, this means the agent should point out the property's position on the flood/inundation map and, ideally, nearby evacuation shelters, before you sign a contract.

The catch: this legal disclosure duty covers flood hazard maps specifically. It does not extend to landslide (土砂災害) zones or tsunami (津波) inundation maps, and it says nothing about foundation-level flood-proofing, drainage history, or how the map has changed since the last update. Checking those layers yourself on disaportal.gsi.go.jp — before the explanation meeting, not during it — is the only way to catch what falls outside the mandatory disclosure.

Why the listing itself won't tell you this

Japanese property listings (物件概要) are built around structural facts — floor area, land category, road frontage, build year — because that is what the listing template and portal fields ask for. Hazard-map status is a separate lookup an agent performs manually at the Important Matters Explanation stage, generally only for flood risk, and generally only once you are already far enough into the process to be sitting across from them. If you are still comparing several akiya candidates by URL, that conversation may be weeks away — which is exactly when it is most useful to already know the answer. The same is true for other risks that don't appear in a standard listing, such as rebuild-prohibited (再建築不可) status, which has nothing to do with hazard maps but is just as absent from the listing headline, or basic questions of whether foreign nationality affects the purchase at all, which it generally does not.

Checklist before you make an offer

  • Look up the exact address on disaportal.gsi.go.jp and record the flood depth band (if any) at the parcel, not just the neighborhood.
  • Switch to the landslide (土砂災害) layer and note whether the lot falls in a yellow zone, red zone, or neither.
  • If the property is within a few kilometers of a coast, check the tsunami (津波) layer separately.
  • Cross-check zoning and any disaster-prevention notes on reinfolib.mlit.go.jp for the same address.
  • Ask the agent directly whether the flood hazard map explanation required at 重要事項説明 has already been prepared, and request to see it before, not just during, that meeting.
  • If the lot is in a red zone (特別警戒区域), ask specifically whether any structural reinforcement conditions apply to future renovation or rebuilding.
  • Save a screenshot of each hazard layer with the date, since maps are periodically updated as new flood and landslide modeling is released.
  • Treat "not shown on the map" as "unmapped or low modeled risk," not as a guarantee — confirm with the municipality's disaster-prevention section for anything borderline.

FAQ

Do I need to read Japanese to use the hazard map portal?

The main hazard map portal (disaportal.gsi.go.jp) and its overlay tool are Japanese-only. You can still use them without reading Japanese by searching the address in Latin letters or pasting coordinates, then reading the color-coded legend, which follows a consistent national color scale for flood depth, landslide zones, and tsunami inundation. A browser translator extension can translate menu labels, but always verify colors and legend values against the original Japanese key.

If a property is not marked on any hazard map, is it risk-free?

No. Hazard maps model expected scenarios based on historical rainfall, river capacity, and topography, but they do not cover every possible event, and some municipalities have not mapped every hazard type. A blank area can mean low modeled risk, or it can mean the map has not been updated with the latest data. Always cross-check the flood, landslide, and tsunami layers separately and confirm directly with the municipality's disaster-prevention section.

Does the seller or agent have to tell me about flood risk?

Since a rule change to the Building Lots and Buildings Transaction Business Act enforcement regulations took effect on August 28, 2020, licensed agents in Japan must show the property's approximate location on the local flood hazard map during the Important Matters Explanation (重要事項説明) before a purchase or lease contract is signed. This covers flood hazard maps only; it does not require disclosure of landslide or tsunami zone status, so buyers should check those layers themselves.
