Juyo jiko setsumei (重要事項説明): the Japanese property disclosure document explained for foreign buyers (2026)

Short answer: the juyo jiko setsumei (重要事項説明, "explanation of important matters", often shortened to 重説, jūsetsu) is the legally required briefing a licensed agent gives you before you sign a purchase contract. A licensed real-estate transaction specialist (宅地建物取引士, takken-shi) walks you through a written document, the 重要事項説明書, that covers ownership, zoning limits, road access, utilities, hazard-map location and the contract terms. It is the most useful single document when buying a house in Japan, including an akiya (空き家). But it is normally in Japanese, it is often handed over close to signing, and in some private sales it does not exist at all.

What do the law and the ministry actually say?

The duty comes from Article 35 of the Real Estate Brokerage Act (宅地建物取引業法, Takken-gyō-hō). In outline: before a sale contract is concluded, the licensed agent must have a takken-shi explain specified matters to the buyer, using a written document the takken-shi signs. The takken-shi must show their licence card (宅地建物取引士証) if you ask. The statute is at e-Gov Law Search, 宅地建物取引業法 (Japanese only).

Two changes matter to buyers today:

  • Flood-hazard location, since 28 August 2020. The agent must show where the property sits on the municipal water-hazard maps (水害ハザードマップ: river flooding, inland-water flooding and storm surge, made under the Flood Control Act) and point out evacuation shelters. The ministry also asks that buyers not be left thinking a location outside a shaded area means zero risk. Source: MLIT press release on the amended enforcement regulations. Our guide to the flood, landslide and tsunami hazard maps shows how to read them yourself.
  • Online explanation and electronic delivery, since 18 May 2022. The explanation can be given by video call (IT重説) and the document delivered electronically, but only with the buyer's consent (承諾). Sources: MLIT, IT重説 and electronic documents and the MLIT implementation manual (PDF, Japanese). For an overseas buyer this can mean a video explanation plus a PDF, but you still need to read Japanese or arrange translation.

When, and by whom, is it explained?

The explanation must come before the purchase contract is concluded (契約成立前), and it must be given by a takken-shi, not by any sales staff member. In practice it is commonly held at the agent's office on the day of signing, often immediately before the contract itself. The statute does not set a number of days, so the safest habit is to ask, in writing, for the draft 重要事項説明書 several days before. That gives you time to translate it, compare it with the listing sheet (マイソク) and ask questions when you are not under pressure. The document you receive on the day is different from the contract (売買契約書); the contract is covered separately under Article 37, and both need reading.

The 3 things buyers miss

1. A private sale may have no 重説 at all

The duty falls on licensed businesses (宅地建物取引業者). If a seller and buyer deal directly, or if a municipal akiya bank only makes an introduction and no licensed agent handles the deal, there may be no statutory disclosure document and no takken-shi obliged to explain anything. Requirements and roles differ by municipality; see our overview of akiya bank conditions for foreigners. If you are in that position, a common approach is to hire a licensed agent or a judicial scrivener (司法書士) for the transaction, and to insist on the same checks in writing.

2. The document is a summary of facts, not a judgment of quality

The 重要事項説明書 records legal and infrastructure facts and whatever the seller and agent know. It does not tell you the roof is sound. Many akiya have no records of asbestos or seismic surveys, which the document will simply state as "none". A house built before 1981 needs its own look; see the pre-1981 seismic standard.

3. It is explained late, in Japanese, and its wording is binding

You may be shown a 15-page document and asked to sign the same hour. The Japanese wording is what counts. A verbal summary in English from a sales representative is not a substitute for reading the text. This is the main risk for foreigners buying a house in Japan: not a missing document, but a document nobody helped them read.

What might it cost to get it translated and checked?

There is no official tariff. Professional translation of a 10 to 20 page real-estate document is usually quoted per page or per character and varies widely between providers, so ask for a fixed quote after sending a sample. A bilingual agent, a judicial scrivener who works in English, or a licensed architect for the physical side each charge separately. Budget for this before the deposit, not after. On the deposit itself: where the seller is a licensed dealer, the Brokerage Act caps the earnest money (手付) at 20% of the price (Article 39, same e-Gov text). That cap does not apply to a private individual seller, so the deposit is whatever the contract says.

How to ask for a translation

  1. Email the agent: 重要事項説明書の案を、契約の数日前にPDFでいただけますか。(Could you send a draft of the explanation of important matters as a PDF a few days before signing?)
  2. Ask for the registry extract (登記事項証明書) and the survey map or boundary documents (測量図) at the same time.
  3. Get a written quote for translation, and ask the translator to keep the Japanese terms beside each English line.
  4. Send questions in writing and keep the answers; that is your paper trail.
  5. If the agent offers IT重説, consent only if you can also receive the document beforehand.

Checklist before you sign

  • Do you have the draft 重要事項説明書 in hand at least several days before signing?
  • Does the registry name the person selling, and are there mortgages or co-owners?
  • Do 用途地域, 建ぺい率, 容積率 and 市街化調整区域 status allow what you plan to do?
  • Does the lot face a road of the required width (接道), and is any set-back needed?
  • Are water, drainage, gas and power connected, and who pays if not?
  • Have you seen your own hazard-map check, not just the agent's page?
  • Are the deposit, cancellation dates and 契約不適合責任 terms clear and in writing?
  • Was a licensed agent involved, and did a takken-shi actually give the explanation?

FAQ

Can I get the juyo jiko setsumei document before the signing day?

The law requires the explanation before the contract is concluded, and does not fix how many days earlier. You can ask the agent in writing to send the draft ahead. Agents commonly accommodate this, but it is a request, not a legal right to a set number of days.

Does a private sale of an akiya without an agent include a juyo jiko setsumei?

Not under the Real Estate Brokerage Act, which binds licensed businesses. If no licensed agent is involved there may be no statutory disclosure document. You can hire a licensed agent or a judicial scrivener to do equivalent checks, and you rely more on the seller's civil-code liability.

Can I ask for the explanation in English?

The document and explanation are normally in Japanese, and the law does not oblige an agent to provide English. Bring an interpreter, look for a bilingual agent, or have the document translated before you sign. The Japanese version is the binding one.
