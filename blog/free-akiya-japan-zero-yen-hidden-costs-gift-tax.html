Free akiya in Japan (0 yen houses): the gift tax, fees and clean-up costs that still apply

Updated 13 October 2026 · Published by the Akiya Check editorial team. General information from public sources, not legal, tax or immigration advice.

Short answer: a house offered for 0 yen is a transfer of ownership without a price, not a house without costs. When an individual gives you property, the receiver is the person who may owe gift tax; when a corporation gives it, the National Tax Agency says income tax is the relevant tax instead. On top of tax there are transfer costs (registration and a prefectural acquisition tax), the cost of whatever is wrong with the house, and the yearly costs of owning it. Free and 0 yen akiya are often free because the owner wants out of those costs. Before you say yes, add up the whole list below. This is general information, not tax advice; confirm the figures with the tax office or a licensed tax professional.

30-second decision

  • The giver is an individual (a relative, heir or private owner): gift tax can apply to you as the receiver once your gifts for the year pass 1.1 million yen. Ask for the tax-assessed value first.
  • The giver is a company: the NTA says income tax, not gift tax, applies to gifts from a corporation. Ask a tax professional how it is treated for you.
  • The house is in poor condition: price the clean-up and demolition before you accept; see the links in the table below.

Why a house is free in the first place

Owners give houses away because owning an empty house is costly and a sale is difficult. Holding costs include the fixed-asset tax, insurance, upkeep and the legal duty to manage the building. Since the Act on Special Measures concerning Vacant Houses was amended (in force from 13 December 2023, per MLIT's announcement), owners also face stronger municipal action against neglected vacant houses; see managing an akiya from overseas. A giver who passes the house on passes those costs on to you. Many municipal akiya banks also list low-priced or free houses; see akiya-bank conditions for foreigners.

Gift tax: who pays and when

The National Tax Agency explains gift tax on its page No. 4402 (gift tax). The points to know:

  • Gift tax is a tax on a person who receives property from an individual as a gift. Receiving a house for free from a private owner can therefore be a taxable gift to you.
  • There is a basic annual deduction of 1.1 million yen. If the total value of gifts you receive in a calendar year is 1.1 million yen or less, no gift tax is due and no return is required.
  • If the total is over that amount, you must file and pay between 1 February and 15 March of the following year.
  • Gifts from a corporation are not subject to gift tax; the NTA page says income tax applies instead.

The value that counts is the value assessed for tax, not the market price and not the 0 yen on the listing. For real estate that is normally linked to the assessed values used for property tax; ask the municipal tax office for the assessed value (固定資産税評価額) of the land and the building, and ask a tax professional how the gift would be valued. Relationship rules and special provisions exist and are outside this guide. If an heir transfers the house to you instead of selling, the position may differ again; ask before you agree.

Transfer costs on top of the tax

Transferring ownership is done by registration at the Legal Affairs Bureau, normally handled by a judicial scrivener (司法書士). The registration tax (登録免許税) depends on the reason for the registration and the assessed value, and the municipality or prefecture may charge a real estate acquisition tax (不動産取得税) after acquisition. We have not verified the current rates or any exemption thresholds for each cause of transfer in this guide, so we do not quote them. Ask the judicial scrivener to list every transfer cost in writing before you accept. If the current owner has died and the registry still shows the old owner, the inheritance registration must be completed first; see inheritance registration, and how to read the registry.

Red flags specific to free houses

  • No registry certificate offered. Get it yourself; it is cheap. Check the giver is the registered owner and whether there are mortgages or seizures.
  • The lot is on a hazard map zone, or is a leasehold or farmland. See leasehold and farmland. A leasehold house is not the land.
  • Pressure to decide fast or to pay a fee to an intermediary before you have seen the contract.
  • The house cannot be rebuilt if it is demolished.

FAQ

Do I pay gift tax if someone gives me an akiya?

It can apply when an individual gives you property and your gifts for the year exceed the 1.1 million yen basic deduction, per the NTA. The value is the assessed value, not the listing price. Gifts from corporations are treated differently (income tax). Ask a tax professional.

Is a 0 yen house a good deal?

It can be, if the lot is sound and you have priced all the costs above. If the house needs demolition or the lot cannot be rebuilt, the total can exceed a bargain-priced house on a good lot.

Can I back out after agreeing to take a free house?

That depends on what you signed. See our cancellation guide for the typical rules on deposits and cancellation in a sale; a gift contract is different, so ask a professional.

Can a foreigner receive a free akiya?

Foreigners can generally own property in Japan; see can foreigners buy akiya.
